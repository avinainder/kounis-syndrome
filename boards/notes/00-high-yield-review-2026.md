## 1. Periprocedural Medications, Contrast, Sedation and CA-AKI

### Metformin (ACR Manual on Contrast Media)

- Risk is lactic acidosis if AKI develops while the drug accumulates (renally cleared, t≤ ~6 h).

- **eGFR ≥30 and no AKI:** no need to stop before or after contrast.

- **Hold at the time of the procedure and for 48 h after, then recheck renal function** if: AKI, eGFR <30, or an intra-arterial study that could embolize to the renal arteries.

- Board default if the stem is vague: hold the morning of the procedure, resume after 48 h with a stable Cr.

### Contrast hypersensitivity

- Anaphylaxis is Type I (IgE/mast cell). Gell–Coombs II = IgG cytotoxic, III = immune complex, IV = T-cell (delayed rash).

- **Elective premedication:** prednisone 50 mg PO at 13, 7 and 1 h before, plus diphenhydramine 50 mg at 1 h. Alternative: methylprednisolone 32 mg PO at 12 and 2 h. **Urgent:** methylprednisolone 40 mg IV or hydrocortisone 200 mg IV (repeat q4h until contrast), plus diphenhydramine 50 mg IV 1 h before. Steroids given <4– 5 h before contrast have little proven effect. H2 blockers and leukotriene antagonists: insufficient evidence.

- Isolated hives or pruritus: diphenhydramine. No epinephrine unless the airway or hemodynamics are involved.

### Anaphylaxis

- **Epinephrine is the only first-line drug.** IM 0.3–0.5 mg (1 mg/mL) into the thigh, every 5–15 min. IV 0.1 mg (0.1 mg/mL) slowly, or 10 mcg boluses, then 1–4 mcg/min infusion.

- **Never give 1 mg IV push** (that is the arrest dose).

- On beta-blockers and refractory: **glucagon 1–5 mg IV over 5 min** (a rapid push causes emesis), then infusion. Adjuncts: IV fluids; albuterol for bronchospasm (not stridor); H1 blocker (skin symptoms only); methylprednisolone 125 mg IV (prevents biphasic reaction, no acute effect).

**UPDATED** Original listed methylprednisolone 12 mg. The dose is 125 mg. “1:1,000 / 1:10,000” labeling has been replaced by 1 mg/mL and 0.1 mg/mL.

### Sedation

**Minimal:** normal response to voice. **Moderate** (most caths): purposeful response to voice or light touch; airway and ventilation maintained. **Deep:** purposeful response only to pain; may need airway support.

- **Flumazenil** 0.2 mg IV over 30 s, repeat to a 1 mg max. Seizure risk in chronic benzodiazepine use.

**Naloxone:** spontaneously breathing 0.04–0.1 mg; apneic 0.4–2 mg; repeat q2–3 min. It is shorter-acting than most opioids, so watch for re-sedation.

Goal of reversal is adequate ventilation, not full arousal.

### PDE5 inhibitors

They potentiate nitrate cGMP, which can cause profound hypotension.

**No nitrates within 24 h of sildenafil or vardenafil, or 48 h of tadalafil** (ACC/AHA).

- Treat hypotension with fluids and an α-agonist (phenylephrine).

### Contrast-associated AKI (CA-AKI)

- **Definitions:** classic CIN = Cr ↑ ≥0.5 mg/dL or ≥25% at 48–72 h. KDIGO = ≥0.3 mg/dL in 48 h or ≥1.5× in 7 d. **Course:** Cr rises at 24–48 h, peaks at 3–5 d, recovers by 7–10 d.

- **Mechanism:** medullary vasoconstriction and hypoxia, reactive oxygen species, direct tubular toxicity. **Patient risk (Mehran):** eGFR <60, DM, age >75, CHF/low EF, hypotension/IABP, anemia, ACS/shock. **Procedural risk:** contrast volume, repeat exposure within 72 h. **Prevention:**

   - Isotonic saline 1–1.5 mL/kg/h for 3–12 h before and 6– 24 h after. Isotonic beats half-normal saline.

   - **POSEIDON** (LVEDP-guided): 3 mL/kg/h for 1 h, then by LVEDP: <13 → 5; 13–18 → 3; >18 → 1.5 mL/kg/h × 4 h. **N-acetylcysteine and bicarbonate: no benefit** (PRESERVE). NAC is Class 3.

Iso-osmolar vs low-osmolar contrast: no consistent difference in modern data.

- **Contrast limits:** maximum dose = 5 mL × weight (kg) / Cr, up to 300 mL. Contrast volume/CrCl >3.7 (some use >2– 3) predicts AKI.

- Minimize volume: biplane, small injections, IVUS-guided minimal-contrast PCI. Stop CTO PCI at >3.7× eGFR.

## 2. Radiation Physics and Safety

### How the system works

- Only ~1% of tube energy becomes x-rays (the rest is heat). The small focal spot is used for fluoro, the large one for cine.

- **kVp** sets beam energy: higher kVp = more penetration, **less contrast **. **mA** sets photon quantity: dose is proportional to mA with no loss of contrast.

- **Pulse width** 4–10 ms; longer causes motion blur.

- **Frame rate:** 7.5 fps fluoro and 15 fps cine are standard. Use 30 fps only for pediatrics or LV imaging.

- **Cu spectral filters** remove low-energy photons that add dose but not image. They improve images and lower dose.

- **Automatic dose-rate control** raises output as attenuation rises (large patient, steep angle).

- **US fluoro limit:** 88 mGy/min (10 R/min); high-level mode 176 mGy/min. **Cine has no legal limit** and ~10× the dose rate of fluoro. Fluoro and cine each contribute roughly half of total dose.

- **Image quality:** contrast (iodine), spatial resolution (blur), noise (quantum mottle; more in fluoro). Anti-scatter grid and flat-panel detector.

### Dose metrics

|**Metric**|**Unit**|**Predicts**|
|---|---|---|
|Air kerma (Ka,r) at the interventional reference point (15 cm toward the tube from isocenter)|mGy|**Deterministic** (skin)|
|Kerma-area product / DAP (K × field area)|Gy·cm²|**Stochastic** (cancer)|
|Effective dose (organ-weighted)|Sv (1 rem = 10 mSv)|Whole-body stochastic risk|

### Biologic effects

**Deterministic effects** have a threshold (skin, cataract). **Stochastic effects** have no threshold (cancer; linear no-threshold model).

**Skin (peak skin dose):**

   - 2 Gy: transient erythema/epilation

   - 5–10 Gy: erythema

   - 10–15 Gy: moist desquamation

   - >15 Gy: ulceration/necrosis (sentinel event)

- Arrange follow-up when Ka,r >5 Gy. **Do not biopsy** a suspected radiation ulcer.

- Thresholds are lower with prior exposure, DM and collagen-vascular disease.

- Cancer risk ≈5% fatal cancer per Sv. **Younger age is the strongest risk modifier **; females have higher risk.

### Occupational limits and protection

**Whole body:** 50 mSv/yr (NRC). Practical goal <5 mSv/yr. **Lens:** NRC 150 mSv/yr; **ICRP recommends 20 mSv/yr **. Skin/extremities: 500 mSv/yr.

- **Declared pregnancy:** fetal limit 5 mSv for the pregnancy (0.5 mSv/month); second dosimeter under the apron. A pregnant worker cannot be excluded from the lab. 0.5 mm Pb apron attenuates ~95–98% of scatter (0.35 mm ~90–95%).

- **The main source of operator dose is patient scatter **, so lowering patient dose lowers operator dose. At 1 m, scatter ≈ 1/1000 of the patient entrance dose; inverse square law applies.

**Reduce dose:**

- Collimate (single best step: less dose, less scatter, better image)

Low frame rate; fluoro-save instead of cine

- Table up and detector down Avoid steep angles and magnification

- Rotate projections to spread skin dose

- Ceiling and table shields close to the patient; step back during cine

- Real-time dose monitoring

Large patients need higher kVp and mA, which means more dose and scatter with worse contrast and more blur.

### Pivotal P2Y12 trials

- **TRITON-TIMI 38:** prasugrel vs clopidogrel (300 mg) in ACS PCI. ↓ CV death/MI/stroke and ST; ↑ major bleeding. Net harm with prior stroke/TIA; no net benefit ≥75 y or <60 kg.

- **PLATO:** ticagrelor vs clopidogrel in ACS. ↓ CV death/MI/stroke, **↓ CV and all-cause death **, ↓ ST; ↑ non-CABG major bleeding.

- **ISAR-REACT 5:** prasugrel > ticagrelor in ACS with an invasive strategy (↓ death/MI/stroke, similar bleeding). **ACCOAST:** prasugrel pretreatment in NSTEMI gave no ischemic benefit and ↑ bleeding.

- **CURRENT-OASIS 7:** double-dose clopidogrel ↓ ST in the PCI subgroup; ↑ bleeding.

   - **TAILOR-PCI** (genotype-guided): primary endpoint NS. **POPular Genetics:** genotype-guided de-escalation noninferior with ↓ bleeding.

   - **Routine platelet-function testing is not recommended** (GRAVITAS, ARCTIC, TRIGGER-PCI).

- **Switching P2Y12 inhibitors (acute/early phase)**

   - **Clopidogrel → ticagrelor:** 180 mg regardless of the last dose.

   - **Clopidogrel → prasugrel:** 60 mg regardless.

   - **Ticagrelor → clopidogrel:** 600 mg 24 h after the last ticagrelor dose.

   - **Ticagrelor → prasugrel:** 60 mg 24 h after the last dose. **Prasugrel → clopidogrel:** 600 mg 24 h later. In the late phase, use maintenance doses.

### ACS (2025 ACC/AHA)

- **Default DAPT is 12 mo** in non-HBR patients (Class 1).

- **Prasugrel or ticagrelor over clopidogrel** for PCI (Class 1). Prasugrel may be preferred in NSTE-ACS (ISAR-REACT 5). **Transition to ticagrelor monotherapy ≥1 mo after PCI** if DAPT is tolerated (Class 1).

- HBR: stop aspirin at 1–3 mo and continue the P2Y12 inhibitor (2a).

- De-escalation from prasugrel/ticagrelor to clopidogrel after 1 mo: 2b.

- Upstream P2Y12 inhibitor only if angiography is delayed >24 h (2b). No routine pretreatment before early cath. GP IIb/IIIa: **routine use Class 3 **; bailout (thrombus, no/slow reflow) 2a. Cangrelor: 2b.

### Chronic coronary disease (2023 CCD guideline)

- Clopidogrel-based DAPT for **6 mo** after PCI.

- HBR: 1–3 mo of DAPT, then P2Y12 inhibitor monotherapy (2a).

- Extended DAPT beyond 12 mo only for high ischemic and low bleeding risk (2b).

- Rivaroxaban 2.5 mg BID + aspirin (COMPASS) for high ischemic / low bleeding risk.

**UPDATED** The original said short-DAPT and aspirin-free strategies were “not in guidelines yet” and to keep aspirin when de-escalating. Both are outdated. P2Y12 monotherapy is now guideline-endorsed.

   - 8–12 h: 0.3 mg/kg IV

   - >12 h or none: 0.5–0.75 mg/kg IV

- **Avoid switching UFH ↔ LMWH** (↑ bleeding; SYNERGY). Anti-Xa goal 0.5–1.0 IU/mL; check it in CKD or at extremes of weight.

- **Fondaparinux** alone causes catheter thrombosis (OASIS-5), so add UFH at PCI.

- **Sheath pull:** ACT <150–180 s; 4–8 h after LMWH; ≥2 h after bivalirudin; fibrinogen >150 mg/dL after lytics. OK to pull on GPI (more oozing).

### Bivalirudin

- **Dosing:** 0.75 mg/kg bolus + 1.75 mg/kg/h. CrCl <30 → 1 mg/kg/h; dialysis → 0.25 mg/kg/h. Not reversed by protamine.

- **HORIZONS-AMI:** ↓ bleeding and mortality vs UFH + GPI, but ↑ acute ST.

- **HEAT-PPCI, MATRIX, VALIDATE-SWEDEHEART:** no advantage over UFH with contemporary radial practice and selective GPI.

- **BRIGHT-4 (2022):** bivalirudin + **high-dose post-PCI infusion (1.75 mg/kg/h for 2–4 h)** ↓ 30-d death/BARC 3–5 bleeding vs UFH in STEMI, without excess ST. Bivalirudin is a guideline alternative to UFH in STEMI PCI and the agent of choice in HIT.

### Heparin-induced thrombocytopenia

- Anti-PF4/heparin IgG. Platelets fall >50% at 5–10 d; rapid onset if heparin exposure within the prior 30–100 d; delayed onset up to 3 wk.

Risk is higher with UFH than LMWH, surgical than medical, and female than male. Untreated thrombosis risk is ~50%. Use the 4T score.

- **Stop all heparin** (including flushes and heparin-coated lines).

- Start a direct thrombin inhibitor: **argatroban** (hepatic; avoid in liver failure; raises INR) or **bivalirudin** (preferred for PCI). Fondaparinux or DOACs are alternatives. Send PF4 ELISA; confirm with serotonin release assay. Warfarin only after platelets ≥150k, overlapping ≥5 d until INR is therapeutic.

- **Duration:** HITT 3 mo; isolated HIT 4–6 wk (up to 3 mo).

### Oral anticoagulants

- **Renal clearance:** dabigatran ~80%, edoxaban ~50%, rivaroxaban ~35% (unchanged), apixaban ~27%.

- **AF dose reductions:**

   - Apixaban 2.5 mg BID if ≥2 of: age ≥80, weight ≤60 kg, Cr ≥1.5

   - Rivaroxaban 15 mg if CrCl ≤50

   - Edoxaban 30 mg if CrCl 15–50 (avoid if CrCl >95) Dabigatran 75 mg BID if CrCl 15–30 (US)

- **VKA still required:** mechanical valves; rheumatic moderate–severe MS with AF (INVICTUS: rivaroxaban inferior); high-risk (triple-positive) APS.

- DOACs are acceptable in obesity, with active cancer (apixaban/edoxaban or LMWH), and increasingly for LV thrombus.

- **VKA pharmacology:** blocks synthesis of factors II, VII, IX, X and proteins C/S. Overlap with a parenteral agent ≥5 d and until INR is therapeutic ≥24 h.

- **Before cath:**

   - Warfarin: **INR ≤2.2 radial, ≤1.8 femoral** (SCAI 2021). No vitamin K.

   - Apixaban/rivaroxaban: hold 24–48 h

   - Dabigatran: 48 h, longer (72–96 h) if CrCl <50

   - Emergency: go radial

- **Bridging:** DOACs need none. VKA bridging only for very high thrombotic risk (mechanical mitral valve, stroke or embolism within 3 mo). BRIDGE trial: no bridging for AF. **Resume OAC** once hemostasis is secure (~4–6 h after radial; next day after femoral). **Before CABG:**

   - Clopidogrel 5 d; prasugrel 7 d; ticagrelor 3–5 d Eptifibatide/tirofiban 2–4 h; cangrelor ~1 h

   - Urgent CABG: hold clopidogrel/ticagrelor ≥24 h if possible

## 4. Bleeding, Vascular Access and Access Complications

### Bleeding

Bleeding is the most common PCI complication and independently predicts mortality.

Access-site bleeding has declined with radial access; nonaccess bleeding (mostly GI) now predominates, especially in ACS.

**BARC:**

- 1: not actionable

- 2: overt, actionable

- 3a: Hb ↓3–<5 g/dL or any transfusion

- 3b: Hb ↓≥5, tamponade, surgery, or vasoactive drugs

- 3c: ICH or intraocular

- 4: CABG-related

- 5: fatal

**Risk factors:** female, **low body weight** (not obesity), older age, CKD, anemia, ACS, prior bleed, thrombocytopenia, OAC/NSAID/steroids, femoral access, high heparin dose, GPI, lytics.

**Avoidance:** radial access; weight-based dosing; avoid UFH↔LMWH crossover; avoid routine GPI; smaller sheaths; ultrasound/fluoro-guided CFA access; early sheath removal; PPI.

### Radial vs femoral

**RIVAL (2011):** no difference in the primary endpoint; benefit in the STEMI subgroup and high-volume centers. **RIFLE-STEACS:** ↓ mortality in STEMI. **MATRIX (2015):** radial ↓ BARC 3/5 bleeding, NACE and all-cause death in ACS. **SAFARI-STEMI (2020):** stopped for futility; neutral mortality.

**Radial is the default **, Class 1 in ACS (↓ bleeding, vascular complications, mortality).

**SIHD:** radial ↓ access bleeding but no mortality benefit. **Radial downsides:** learning curve; slightly more radiation/contrast early; longer door-to-balloon (minutes); crossover ~5–10%.

**Distal radial (DISCO RADIAL, 2022):** RAO not significantly lower (0.3% vs 0.9%); higher crossover.

**Allen/Barbeau testing is not required** before radial access (RADAR: no hand ischemia). Ultrasound improves first-pass success (RAUST).

- **Radial artery occlusion (RAO) and radial troubleshooting**

   - **RAO** occurs in ~1–5% (up to 10%). Risk: female, small artery, large sheath, spasm, low heparin, occlusive hemostasis.

**Prevention:** heparin ≥50 U/kg or 5,000 U (give even if INR is therapeutic); **patent hemostasis** (PROPHET); prophylactic ulnar compression (PROPHET-II); smaller/hydrophilic sheaths.

**RAO treatment:** ipsilateral ulnar compression 1 h or anticoagulation (LMWH ~4 wk). About half recanalize spontaneously.

- **Anatomic variants:** radial loop (almost always with an accessory recurrent radial; cross with 0.014" wire or hydrophilic wire, then straighten); high-bifurcating radial (small vessel, spasm); subclavian/innominate tortuosity (age ≥75, short stature, HTN; deep inspiration or stiff wire; consider left radial).

- **Forearm perforation:** keep going. Cross with a 0.014" wire, balloon-assisted tracking, and seal with a long sheath or catheter; compressive wrap afterward.

- **Catheter kink/knot** (no torque transmission, damped trace): **do not pull **. Fix the catheter proximally (BP cuff, manual pressure) and untwist; straighten with a wire; snare from femoral access.

**Severe spasm/entrapment:** sedation, IA verapamil/NTG, SC nitroglycerin, warm compress; **inflate a brachial BP cuff 5 min then release** (reactive hyperemia); deep sedation or anesthesia if needed.

- **Radial cocktail:** verapamil 2.5–5 mg ± NTG 100–200 mcg + heparin.

- **Radial pseudoaneurysm:** compression band; thrombin or surgery if it fails.

### Femoral access

- **Target:** CFA over the middle of the femoral head, below the inferior epigastric artery (IEA) and above the bifurcation.

- **High stick** (above IEA) → retroperitoneal bleed. **Low stick** (SFA/profunda) → pseudoaneurysm or AV fistula. **Ultrasound guidance** (FAUST): ↓ attempts, venipuncture and time to access. Improves CFA cannulation mainly with high bifurcations. Standard of care with fluoroscopic landmarking.

- **Emergency blind stick:** ~2 cm below the midpoint of the ASIS–pubic tubercle line.

- **Access-complication risk:** female, age ≥65, low weight or BMI ≥40, HTN, CKD, CHF, PAD, AMI, sheath size and dwell time, anticoagulation.

### Specific complications

**Retroperitoneal hematoma** (0.15–0.75%)

- Hypotension/tachycardia, flank/back/lower abdominal pain; bradycardia may occur. ~75% present within 3 h.

- Treat before imaging if unstable: manual compression, stop/reverse anticoagulation, fluids/blood. CT if stable.

- Persistent bleeding: contralateral access, balloon tamponade, covered stent or coil; surgery rarely.

### Pseudoaneurysm

Pulsatile tender mass with bruit.

<2 cm: often closes spontaneously; rescan in ~2 wk.

- ≥2–3 cm, symptomatic, expanding or on anticoagulation: **US-guided thrombin injection** (~97% success; best for narrow neck).

- US compression: 75–98% success, painful, lower success on anticoagulation.

- Surgery: infected, rapidly expanding, skin necrosis, or neuropathy.

### AV fistula

   - Continuous bruit/thrill; low stick; left-sided; simultaneous artery and vein access.

   - ~⅓ close by 1 yr; observe small ones.

   - Large (high-output HF, steal, limb edema): covered stent or surgery.

- **Dissection:** retrograde flaps usually seal with antegrade flow. Flow-limiting → PTA/stent.

### Vascular closure devices

   - Shorten time to ambulation; **do not ↓ bleeding or vascular complications** in RCTs. Device failure raises complications ~3×.

   - Avoid with: puncture above IEA or at/below bifurcation, CFA <5 mm, heavy calcification/PAD, infection risk, collagen or bovine allergy (Angio-Seal). Infection is usually a **mycotic pseudoaneurysm **.

- **Brachial access:** higher complication rate (thrombosis, median nerve compression from hematoma). Avoid closure devices.

## 5. Coronary Pathobiology, MI Definitions and Coronary Anomalies

### Atherosclerosis

**Sequence:**

   - Endothelial dysfunction (↓ NO; earliest step, independently prognostic)

   - LDL entry and oxidation

   - Monocyte adhesion → macrophage → foam cell (fatty streak)

   - VSMC migration and collagen synthesis (fibrous cap) Necrotic core and vasa vasorum neovascularization (intraplaque hemorrhage)

- **Vulnerable plaque (TCFA):** cap <65 µm, large lipid core, macrophages/T cells, few VSMCs, MMP activity, positive remodeling, spotty calcium. **Rupture occurs at the cap shoulder.**

- **Remodeling index:** lesion EEM area / reference EEM area. Positive >1.05 (ACS, vulnerable); negative <0.95 (elderly, DM, stable). Early plaque grows outward, so the angiogram looks normal.

**ACS substrates:**

- **Rupture** (~60–75%): TCFA; more often STEMI.

- **Erosion** (~25–40%): intact cap, SMC- and proteoglycanrich, few inflammatory cells; young women and smokers; more often NSTE-ACS. OCT diagnosis; some managed without stenting (EROSION). **Calcified nodule** (~5%): elderly, CKD, DM; tortuous RCA and LM.

Most ACS arises from non-obstructive (<50%) plaques because they vastly outnumber severe lesions, although each severe lesion carries higher individual risk. 20–75% of ACS patients have multiple ruptured plaques (the “vulnerable patient”).

**Vasomotor testing:** acetylcholine is endothelium- _dependent_ (dilates normal arteries, constricts dysfunctional ones). Adenosine, NTG and nitroprusside are endothelium- _independent_.

- **Neoatherosclerosis** develops earlier and more often in DES (months to 1–2 yr) than BMS (>2–5 yr).

### Inflammation trials

**CANTOS:** canakinumab (IL-1β) ↓ MACE 15%, ↑ fatal infection; LDL unchanged.

**CIRT:** low-dose methotrexate neutral (IL-6/CRP not lowered).

   - **COLCOT** (post-MI) and **LoDoCo2** (CCD): colchicine ↓ MACE; more GI effects and a non-CV death signal. **CLEAR SYNERGY (2024):** colchicine after MI had no MACE benefit.

   - Colchicine 0.5 mg is FDA-approved; guidelines give it 2b.

- **Universal definition of MI (4th)**

   - **Type 1:** atherothrombotic.

   - **Type 2:** supply/demand mismatch. Worse prognosis, driven by non-cardiac death.

- **Type 3:** cardiac death before biomarkers.

- **Type 4a** (PCI-related, ≤48 h):

   - Normal baseline: cTn >5× the 99th percentile URL. Elevated but stable/falling baseline: >20% rise to >5× URL.

   - Plus new ischemic ECG changes, angiographic complication, or new imaging loss of viable myocardium.

- **Type 4b:** stent thrombosis. **Type 4c:** restenosis. **Type 5** (CABG): >10× URL plus new Q waves, graft occlusion, or imaging evidence.

- **SCAI definition** (prognostically significant periprocedural MI): CK-MB ≥10× ULN (≥5× with new Q waves) or cTn ≥70×.

Pretreatment with antiplatelet therapy and a highintensity statin load ↓ periprocedural MI.

### SCAD, vasospasm and INOCA

**SCAD:** ~90% women; peripartum, FMD, extreme exertion/emotion. Look for an abrupt caliber change or long smooth narrowing (type 2).

   - **Conservative management preferred** (most heal). PCI only for ongoing ischemia, instability or LM/proximal involvement (high failure rate).

   - Prefer IVUS over OCT (contrast flush can propagate). Screen for FMD. Beta-blockers ↓ recurrence.

- **Vasospasm:** VSMC hyperreactivity ± endothelial dysfunction; East Asian ancestry, smoking, 5-FU, cocaine. Treat with CCB ± nitrates; avoid non-selective BB. **INOCA/MVD thresholds:** CFR <2.0–2.5, IMR ≥25, abnormal ACh test.

   - Epicardial spasm: ≥90% constriction with symptoms and ECG changes.

   - Microvascular spasm: symptoms and ECG changes without epicardial spasm.

- **CorMicA:** stratified therapy guided by invasive testing improved angina.

Non-invasive assessment: PET or CMR myocardial blood flow reserve (2a).

### Coronary anomalies

Seen in 0.6–1.5% of angiograms; ~80% benign. **Second leading cause of SCD in young athletes** (after HCM). SCD is often the first event, typically during or after exertion. Stress testing is unreliable because obstruction is dynamic.

**Most common:** separate LAD/LCx ostia, then **LCx from the right sinus or RCA** (retroaortic, benign). **Clues:** long LM without LCx filling; an unperfused territory.

**High-risk: anomalous LEFT from the right sinus with an interarterial course.** Other high-risk features are slit-like ostium, acute take-off angle, intramural segment (length) and lateral compression on IVUS. Anomalous RCA from the left sinus is more common but lower risk.

- **Anomalous LM courses from the right sinus:**

   - **Interarterial:** normal-length LAD; “anterior dot” in RAO; dangerous.

   - **Retroaortic:** “posterior dot”; benign.

   - **Septal (intramyocardial):** gives septal branches; short LAD.

   - **Prepulmonic:** short LAD.

- **Work-up:** CCTA (or CMR in young patients to avoid radiation), ischemia testing, IVUS.

- **Treatment:**

   - **Surgery (unroofing preferred)** for anomalous left or right coronary with ischemia or symptoms (Class 1). Reasonable for asymptomatic anomalous LEFT with interarterial/intramural course, especially young or athletic patients.

   - Anomalous RCA without symptoms or ischemia: observation, shared decision-making.

   - Bypass grafts fail from competitive flow; reimplantation risks ostial stenosis. PCI reserved for poor surgical candidates.

- **Engagement:** RCA from left sinus → AL1/AL2, JL; LCx from right sinus → JR4, AR, MP; LM from right sinus → JL/AL. **Coronary fistula:** congenital or acquired (PCI, CABG, biopsy, ablation, myectomy). Origin RCA ≈ LAD > LCx; >90% drain to the right heart/PA.

- Close (catheter or surgery) if large, or small–moderate with ischemia, arrhythmia, ventricular enlargement/dysfunction, or endocarditis. Small asymptomatic: no closure; follow every 3–5 yr.

- **ALCAPA** (Bland–White–Garland): infantile form (~85– 90%, HF with feeding) vs adult form (RCA collaterals → steal, MR, VT, SCD). Surgical reimplantation.

- **Myocardial bridge:** BB ± non-DHP CCB (avoid nitrates), ± ivabradine. Unroofing/myotomy if refractory. **Avoid stenting** (perforation, fracture, ISR).

- **Ectasia:** ≥1.5× reference diameter. ACS with ectasia and thrombus: antithrombotic medical therapy.

### IVUS and OCT

### Outcome evidence

**IVUS-XPL** and **ULTIMATE:** IVUS-guided DES ↓ TVF. Meeting ULTIMATE optimization criteria lowered TVF further.

- **ADAPT-DES** (registry): IVUS benefit greatest in ACS and complex disease.

**RENOVATE-COMPLEX-PCI (2023):** imaging-guided complex PCI ↓ TVF (7.7% vs 12.3%).

- **OCTOBER (2023):** OCT-guided complex bifurcation PCI ↓ MACE.

- **ILUMIEN IV (2023):** OCT ↑ MSA and ↓ ST, but TVF was not significantly lower.

- **OCTIVUS:** OCT noninferior to IVUS.

- **IVUS-ACS (2024):** ↓ TVF in ACS. **Network meta-analysis (2024):** imaging guidance ↓ cardiac death, MI, ST and TVR.

- **Guidelines:** Class 1 for LM and complex lesions (2025 ACS; ESC 2024 CCS). ACC/AHA 2021: 2a for PCI guidance, 2a for mechanism of stent failure.

**UPDATED** The original cited 2011 IIa/IIb recommendations and said “OCT has less data.” Imagingguided PCI now carries a Class 1 recommendation for complex disease.

### Optimization targets

**ULTIMATE:** MSA >5.0 mm² (IVUS) or >90% of distal reference lumen; edge plaque burden <50% within 5 mm; no edge dissection involving media >3 mm.

**OCT:** MSA >4.5 mm²; expansion >80–90%. **LM “5-6-7-8” rule (IVUS MSA):** ostial LCx 5, ostial LAD 6, polygon of confluence 7, proximal LM 8 mm².

- **ISR/ST predictors:** MSA <5.5 mm² (IVUS), stent length >40 mm, underexpansion, geographic miss, edge dissection, tissue protrusion. Acute malapposition alone is not predictive.

**OCT calcium score** (arc >180° = 2, thickness >0.5 mm = 1, length >5 mm = 1): score 4 predicts underexpansion, so modify the calcium first.

### Imaging for lesion severity

**LM:** IVUS MLA ≥6 mm² → safe to defer (LITRO; ~FFR 0.80 in Western patients; ~4.5–4.8 mm² in Asian series). MLA <6 → revascularize or confirm with physiology.

   - **Non-LM:** MLA ≥4 mm² identifies non-ischemic lesions, but **a small MLA does not prove ischemia **. Use physiology.

   - **FLAVOUR (2022):** FFR-guided PCI noninferior to IVUSguided for intermediate (40–70%) lesions, with fewer PCIs.

   - Vulnerable-plaque features (PROSPECT): plaque burden >70%, MLA <4 mm², TCFA. **PREVENT (2024):** preventive PCI of vulnerable non-flow-limiting plaque ↓ targetvessel events; not yet guideline practice.

### Coronary physiology

### Indices and cutoffs

**FFR** = Pd/Pa at maximal hyperemia (full form (Pd−Pv)/(Pa−Pv); subtract RA pressure if RA is markedly elevated).

**≤0.80** = ischemic (older 0.75 validation).

- Lesion-specific and epicardial; independent of HR, BP and contractility.

- Lower FFR (<0.60) = higher event risk and larger benefit.

**Non-hyperemic pressure ratios** (iFR, RFR, dPR): **≤0.89 **. No adenosine; wave-free diastole. iFR pullback with coregistration predicts post-PCI result.

**Angiography-derived FFR (QFR):** FAVOR III China beat angiography, but **FAVOR III Europe (2024): QFR inferior to FFR **.

**CFR** = hyperemic/resting flow (Doppler or thermodilution). Integrates epicardial and microvascular function. <2.0 abnormal (<2.5 for MVD); normal 3–5.

- **IMR** ≥25 = microvascular dysfunction.

**FFR/iFR discordance:** FFR+/iFR− → focal lesion with preserved hyperemic flow. FFR−/iFR+ → diffuse disease or impaired microvascular reserve. Deferred discordant lesions do well.

### Key trials

**DEFER:** PCI of FFR ≥0.75 lesions gave no benefit at 5 and 15 yr; deferral is safe. **FAME:** FFR-guided multivessel PCI ↓ death/MI/revascularization with fewer stents. Intermediate (50–70%) angiographic lesions were most often misclassified.

**FAME 2:** FFR ≤0.80 → PCI + OMT ↓ urgent revascularization (not death/MI) vs OMT. Deferred FFRnegative lesions had low event rates.

- **FAME 3** (3VD): 1 yr, FFR-PCI failed noninferiority to CABG (MACCE; repeat revascularization). **5 yr: death/stroke/MI 16% vs 14% (NS) **; MI and repeat revascularization higher with PCI.

**DEFINE-FLAIR** and **iFR-SWEDEHEART** (2017): iFR noninferior at 1 yr with fewer revascularizations and less discomfort. **5-yr DEFINE-FLAIR:** MACE NS, but all-cause death 9.0% vs 6.2% (HR 1.56) with iFR; iFR-SWEDEHEART showed no difference.

- **Guidelines** (2021 ACC/AHA): FFR or iFR is **Class 1** for intermediate stenosis in SIHD without prior ischemia testing.

**UPDATED** Add the 5-yr FAME 3 convergence and the unexplained 5-yr iFR mortality signal. Both are now commonly tested.

### STEMI non-culprit physiology

**DANAMI-3-PRIMULTI** and **COMPARE-ACUTE:** FFR-guided complete revascularization ↓ repeat revascularization vs culprit-only. Non-culprit FFR is safe acutely. **FLOWER-MI (2021):** FFR- vs angiography-guided complete revascularization: no difference; fewer stents with FFR.

- **FRAME-AMI (2023):** FFR-guided better than angiographyguided.

**FULL REVASC (2024):** FFR-guided complete revascularization did not ↓ death/MI/unplanned revascularization vs culprit-only.

In acute STEMI, microvascular stunning can cause **falsenegative FFR **. Non-culprit assessment is reasonable; culprit FFR is not valid.

### Pitfalls

**False-negative (FFR too high):**

- Inadequate hyperemia (caffeine, theophylline, low IC dose)

- Guide damping/deep engagement (disengage during measurement)

Guide side holes with IC adenosine

- Large guide

Severe microvascular dysfunction (acute STEMI, LVH >13 mm, LVEF <30%)

Small territory or non-viable myocardium

Pre-TAVR severe AS (FFR may fall after TAVR; iFR less affected)

**False-positive (FFR too low):**

   - **Drift:** check pressure waveforms. A true distal gradient loses the dicrotic notch; drift keeps a similar shape. Pull the sensor back to the guide tip and re-equalize (target 1.00 ± 0.02).

   - Wire-induced pseudostenosis (accordion)

   - Spasm (always give IC NTG)

   - Contrast in the guide (flush with saline)

   - Sensor placed too distal in a diffusely diseased vessel

- **Serial lesions:** hyperemic pullback, treat the largest stepup first, then re-measure (lesions interact).

**Adenosine:** IV 140 mcg/kg/min or IC boluses (RCA ~50– 100 mcg, LCA ~100–200 mcg). Watch for AV block in the RCA. Dipyridamole potentiates adenosine.

## 7. Chronic Coronary Disease, Revascularization, Left Main and Bifurcation

### Testing (2021 chest pain / 2023 CCD guidelines)

### No known CAD

- **Low risk:** defer testing (Class 1), or CAC / exercise ECG (2a).

- **Intermediate–high risk:** CCTA (favored <65 y, not on OMT) or stress imaging (favored ≥65 y or known CAD burden). PET > SPECT (2a).

- Exercise ECG is reasonable if the ECG is interpretable and the patient can do ≥5 METs.

- CT-FFR is reasonable for 40–90% proximal/mid stenosis.

- Add CAC to stress imaging (2a). CAC or CCTA in asymptomatic screening without indication: Class 3.

### Known CAD

   - Stress imaging for new or worsening symptoms (Class 1).

   - CCTA reasonable for stents ≥3 mm, grafts, or nonobstructive CAD progression.

   - **No routine periodic stress testing** when stable.

- **High-risk non-invasive findings:** ≥10% ischemic myocardium (SPECT/PET), ≥3 segments on stress echo, CMR ≥12% or ≥3 segments, TID, exercise hypotension. **Go to invasive angiography:** CCTA showing ≥50% LM or ≥70% 3-vessel disease with symptoms (Class 1); or persistent limiting angina / high-risk ischemia on GDMT. Physiology if no prior testing.

- **Viability:** LGE extent has best sensitivity for recovery; dobutamine CMR best specificity. **STICH:** viability did not predict CABG benefit. Stunning = transient dysfunction with normal flow; hibernation = chronic hypoperfusion.

### Medical therapy

- BB or CCB first-line for angina, then long-acting nitrate or ranolazine.

- **Ranolazine:** late Na⁺ current inhibitor; no HR/BP effect; QT prolongation; CYP3A interactions.

- **Long-term BB without a specific indication** (MI within ~1 yr, LVEF ≤50%, arrhythmia, angina) does not ↓ events. BB benefit after MI with preserved EF is contested: REDUCE-AMI and REBOOT neutral, BETAMI-DANBLOCK favorable.

Vasospastic angina: CCB + nitrates.

- High-intensity statin. **2026 dyslipidemia guideline:** LDL <55 mg/dL for very-high-risk ASCVD, <70 for others; add ezetimibe, then PCSK9i / inclisiran / bempedoic acid.

SGLT2i and GLP-1 RA in DM or obesity; BP <130/80; icosapent ethyl if TG 135–499 on statin (REDUCE-IT). Colchicine 2b. EECP 2b.

### Revascularization trials

- **COURAGE (2007):** PCI + OMT did not ↓ death/MI in SIHD.

- **ISCHEMIA (2020)** (moderate–severe ischemia; excluded LM ≥50%, LVEF <35%, recent ACS, unacceptable angina): no ↓ death/MI; **better angina/QoL **. ISCHEMIA-EXTEND: ↓ CV death, ↑ non-CV death, all-cause similar.

- **REVIVED-BCIS2 (2022):** PCI in LVEF ≤35% with viability did not ↓ death/HF hospitalization.

- **STICH/STICHES:** CABG ↓ all-cause death at 10 yr in ischemic cardiomyopathy (LVEF ≤35%).

- **FREEDOM:** DM with multivessel disease, CABG ↓ death/MI; ↑ stroke.

- **SYNTAX:** PCI failed noninferiority. CABG better for SYNTAX ≥33; LM subgroup similar (more revascularization with PCI, more stroke with CABG).

- **EXCEL** (LM, SYNTAX ≤32): 3-yr noninferior. 5-yr primary NS (22.0% vs 19.2%), but **all-cause death higher with PCI (13.0% vs 9.9%) **, plus more ischemia-driven revascularization.

- **NOBLE** (LM): 5-yr MACCE worse with PCI (28% vs 19%), driven by non-procedural MI and repeat revascularization; no mortality difference.

- **Individual-patient meta-analysis (11 RCTs):** CABG ↓ mortality in multivessel disease with DM or high SYNTAX; similar in LM with low–intermediate SYNTAX.

### Guideline recommendations (ACC/AHA 2021 revascularization, 2023 CCD)

- **Heart team** for complex multivessel/LM disease, DM or LV dysfunction (Class 1).

**To improve survival:**

   - Significant LM → CABG (Class 1); PCI reasonable for low–intermediate complexity LM (2a). Multivessel CAD with LVEF ≤35% → CABG (Class 1). DM + multivessel CAD including LAD → CABG with LIMA (Class 1); PCI if poor surgical candidate (2b). 3VD with normal EF → CABG 2b; PCI benefit uncertain. Complex multivessel disease (SYNTAX >33) → CABG over PCI (2a).

- **For symptoms:** revascularize refractory angina despite GDMT (Class 1). Patients should be on adequate antianginal therapy first.

**DES over BMS in all** (Class 1), including HBR.

- **Risk scores:** SYNTAX low ≤22, intermediate 23–32, high ≥33 (anatomic complexity, irrelevant to surgeons). STS PROM low <4%, intermediate 4–8%, high >8%. If on the fence and the patient has AF with high HASBLED, favor CABG (avoids triple therapy).

### Appropriate use in SIHD (condensed)

- **Rarely appropriate:** 1–2-vessel disease without proximal LAD, asymptomatic or not on antianginals, with low-risk findings.

- **Appropriate:** symptoms on ≥1–2 antianginals, or highrisk ischemia.

- **3VD / LM:** CABG appropriate across scenarios. LM PCI rarely appropriate with high SYNTAX in asymptomatic patients.

- **“Severe” for AUC:** ≥70% non-LM (≥50% LM), or 40–69% with FFR ≤0.80 / iFR ≤0.89.

**NCDR CathPCI metrics:** risk-adjusted mortality, bleeding, AKI, 30-d readmission, discharge meds (aspirin/P2Y12/statin), radiation dose, cardiac rehab referral, STEMI door-to-device times, AUC. Outliers often reflect documentation or abstraction errors.

### Left main assessment

- Angiography is unreliable (short segment, no reference, ostial catheter effects, overlap).

- **IVUS MLA <6 mm²** or **FFR ≤0.80 / iFR ≤0.89** is significant. Downstream LAD/LCx disease can falsely raise LM FFR. Measure in the non-diseased daughter or treat the distal lesion first.

**LM STEMI or shock → emergency PCI.** Elective LM is never ad hoc; heart team.

### Bifurcation PCI

Plaque forms on the lateral wall (low shear) and spares the carina. **Carina shift **, more than plaque shift, pinches the side branch after main-vessel stenting; most pinches are not FFR-significant.

- **Medina (MB prox, MB distal, SB):** 1 = ≥50% stenosis. Descriptive, not prognostic.

- **Provisional stenting is the default** (Class 1):

   - Wire both branches (jailed SB wire).

   - Stent the MV sized to distal MV.

   - **POT** with a short NC balloon sized to the proximal MV. Treat SB only for TIMI <3, severe dissection, or severe ostial compromise with ischemia (T/TAP or culotte).

- **Planned 2-stent** for **complex bifurcations (DEFINITION criteria) **:

   - **Major:** SB lesion length ≥10 mm AND SB DS ≥70% (distal LM) or ≥90% (non-LM).

   - **Minor:** moderate–severe calcification, multiple lesions, angle <45° or >70°, MV RVD <2.5 mm, thrombus, MV lesion ≥25 mm.

   - **Complex = 1 major + ≥2 minor.**

- **Trials:**

   - **DKCRUSH-V:** DK crush ↓ TLF vs provisional in true distal LM bifurcations.

   - **DEFINITION II:** systematic 2-stent (mostly DK crush) ↓ TLF in complex bifurcations.

- **EBC MAIN (2021):** provisional ≈ planned 2-stent in distal LM at 1 and 3 yr; simpler and cheaper.

- **EXCEL/NOBLE:** most distal LM treated provisionally.

**DK crush steps:**

- Wire both; SB stent with 2–3 mm protrusion; balloon crush.

- Rewire through a proximal cell; 1st kissing.

- MV stent; POT.

Rewire; 2nd kissing (NC balloons); final POT.

- In 2-stent techniques, **final kissing with NC balloons is mandatory **. Imaging is essentially mandatory in LM.

## 8. Acute Coronary Syndromes, Cardiogenic Shock and MCS

### NSTE-ACS: risk and timing (2025 ACC/AHA)

ECG within 10 min; hs-troponin 0/1–2 h pathways. Troponin level correlates with risk even below the MI cutoff.

- **TIMI score (1 point each):** age ≥65; ≥3 risk factors; known CAD ≥50%; aspirin in the past 7 d; ≥2 angina episodes in 24 h; ST deviation ≥0.5 mm; positive biomarker.

**GRACE >140** = high risk.

- **Timing of invasive strategy:**

**Immediate (<2 h):** refractory angina, hemodynamic or electrical instability, acute HF, mechanical complication.

- **Early (<24 h):** high risk (GRACE >140, dynamic ST changes, rising troponin) (2a). TIMACS: benefit of early cath limited to GRACE >140.

- **Invasive during hospitalization** for intermediate/high risk (Class 1).

- **Selective invasive** for low risk (negative troponin, no ST change); Class 3 for early invasive with low likelihood of ACS or prohibitive comorbidity.

- **SENIOR-RITA (2024):** invasive strategy in NSTEMI ≥75 y ↓ non-fatal MI, no mortality difference.

- **Avoid:** IV BB if shock risk (COMMIT); O₂ unless SpO₂ <90% (DETO2X); NSAIDs; excess morphine (delays P2Y12 absorption).

- **Transfusion (MINT 2023):** restrictive strategy not noninferior. Hb ≥10 may be reasonable (2b).

- **Complete revascularization** is Class 1 in NSTE-ACS (angiography- or physiology-guided).

**UPDATED** The original used 2014 timing tiers (72 h), “Hb threshold 8,” and Class 1 for GPI if not preloaded. See the 2025 recommendations above.

### STEMI reperfusion

- **Primary PCI targets:** FMC-to-device ≤90 min at a PCIcapable center; ≤120 min with transfer (door-in-door-out ≤30 min).

- Each 30-min delay ↑ 1-yr mortality ~7.5% (relative).

- Primary PCI beats lytics (↓ death, reinfarction, stroke).

- **Fibrinolysis** if PCI is not achievable within 120 min and symptoms ≤12 h (Class 1). Give within 30 min of arrival (≤10 min of diagnosis prehospital).

- 12–24 h with ongoing ischemia, large territory or instability: lysis 2a if no PCI available.

- **Lysis for ST depression: harm (Class 3) **, except posterior MI.

**Absolute contraindications to lysis:**

   - Any prior ICH

   - Structural cerebrovascular lesion (AVM, aneurysm) or malignant intracranial neoplasm

   - Ischemic stroke within 3 mo (except acute stroke within 4.5 h)

   - Suspected aortic dissection

   - Active bleeding or bleeding diathesis (not menses) Significant closed-head/facial trauma within 3 mo

   - Intracranial/intraspinal surgery within 2 mo

   - Severe uncontrolled HTN unresponsive to therapy

- **Lytic facts:** ICH ~1%. ~50–60% achieve TIMI 3 flow by 90 min. Reocclusion/reinfarction 5–10%.

- **Adjuncts with lysis:**

   - Aspirin 162–325 mg.

   - **Clopidogrel 300 mg (≤75 y) or 75 mg (>75 y) **, continued ≥14 d up to 12 mo. **TREAT:** switching to ticagrelor after lysis (<75 y) is noninferior for bleeding. Anticoagulation ≥48 h, ideally for the hospitalization (≤8 d) or until revascularization.

   - **Enoxaparin preferred** (ExTRACT-TIMI 25: ↓ death/MI vs UFH). <75 y: 30 mg IV + 1 mg/kg SC q12h. ≥75 y: no bolus, 0.75 mg/kg q12h. CrCl <30: daily.

   - UFH: aPTT 1.5–2× control for 48 h. Fondaparinux acceptable, but not alone for PCI.

   - STREAM: half-dose TNK in ≥75 y (ICH).

- **After lysis:**

   - **Failed reperfusion** (<50% ST resolution at 60–90 min, ongoing pain, instability) → **immediate rescue PCI** (Class 1).

   - Successful → **angiography within 2–24 h** (Class 1, pharmacoinvasive).

   - STREAM: prehospital TNK within 3 h + planned cath ≈ primary PCI outcomes (~1/3 needed urgent cath). **Facilitated PCI** (immediate PCI right after full-dose lytic) is harmful.

**Primary PCI details:**

   - **Routine aspiration thrombectomy: Class 3** (TASTE, TOTAL: ↑ stroke). Bailout only.

   - Deferred stenting has no benefit (DANAMI-3-DEFER). UFH, or bivalirudin (BRIGHT-4 regimen). GPI bailout only.

- **OAT:** stable patients >24 h (days 3–28) with an occluded IRA and no ischemia: no benefit to PCI (Class 3).

### Complete revascularization in STEMI

**PRAMI, CvLPRIT, DANAMI-3-PRIMULTI, COMPAREACUTE, COMPLETE (2019):** non-culprit PCI ↓ CV death/MI (COMPLETE) vs culprit-only.

**Class 1** for hemodynamically stable STEMI.

**Timing:**

- **MULTISTARS AMI (2023):** immediate multivessel PCI noninferior (fewer MI/unplanned revascularizations) vs staged at 19–45 d.

**BIOVASC:** immediate ≈ staged in ACS.

   - 2025 guideline has a slight preference for singleprocedure; staging within 45 d is acceptable.

- **FIRE (2023):** physiology-guided complete revascularization in ≥75 y ↓ events.

- Complex residual disease (high SYNTAX) → heart team (staged CABG).

- **Cardiogenic shock: culprit-only at index PCI** (CULPRITSHOCK: ↓ 30-d death/RRT). Routine non-culprit PCI at index is Class 3 (harm).

### Cardiogenic shock

**Epidemiology:** complicates ~5–10% of STEMI (less NSTEMI). Leading cause of in-hospital AMI death; mortality 40–50%. Often develops after admission. 70– 80% have multivessel disease.

- **NSTEMI-shock:** older, more comorbidity, often an occluded LCx.

- **Hemodynamics:**

   - SBP <90 ≥30 min or pressors needed; CI <1.8 unsupported (<2.2 supported); PCWP >15; RAP >10.

   - **CPO** = MAP × CO / 451; **<0.6 W** is the strongest hemodynamic mortality predictor (SHOCK).

   - **PAPi** = (PASP − PADP)/RAP; <0.9–1.0 = RV failure.

   - RAP/PCWP >0.8 = RV-predominant.

- **SCAI SHOCK stages:**

   - **A** at risk

   - **B** beginning: hypotension/tachycardia without hypoperfusion

   - **C** classic: hypoperfusion needing a pressor, inotrope or MCS; lactate ≥2

   - **D** deteriorating despite initial therapy

   - **E** extremis: CPR/ECMO, lactate ≥5, pH ≤7.2

**2025 guideline:**

   - Emergency culprit revascularization regardless of age (Class 1).

   - Microaxial flow pump in selected STEMI-CS (2a).

   - **Routine IABP or VA-ECMO: Class 3 (no benefit).** Transfer to a shock center; MCS as bridge for mechanical complications (2a).

- **Pressors: norepinephrine first **. Inotropes: dobutamine or milrinone (DOREMI: similar). Dopamine ↑ arrhythmias (SOAP II). Epinephrine ↑ refractory shock (OptimaCC). PA catheter for unclear or deteriorating cases.

- Red flags: lactate ≥4, Cr ≥2, CPO <0.6, age ≥70, OHCA.

- **Trials:**

   - **SHOCK (1999):** emergency revascularization — 30-d NS, **↓ 6-mo and long-term mortality **.

   - **IABP-SHOCK II:** no benefit at 30 d, 1 yr or 6 yr.

   - **ECLS-SHOCK (2023): VA-ECMO did not ↓ 30-d mortality **; ↑ bleeding and vascular complications. **DanGer Shock (2024):** Impella CP in STEMI-CS (excluded comatose OHCA and RV failure) **↓ 180-d death (45.8% vs 58.5%) **; ↑ bleeding, limb ischemia, RRT.

### Cardiac arrest

- **TTM2 (2021):** 33 °C no better than normothermia with fever prevention.

- **2025 AHA:** deliberate temperature control (32–37.5 °C) for **≥36 h** in comatose patients.

- **COACT/TOMAHAWK:** no benefit of immediate angiography after OHCA without ST elevation. Immediate cath is for STEMI or ongoing instability.

### MCS for high-risk PCI

- **BCIS-1:** elective IABP gave no ↓ MACCE (later long-term mortality signal).

- **PROTECT II:** Impella 2.5 vs IABP — 30-d NS; 90-d per-protocol trend favoring Impella.

- **CHIP-BCIS3 (ACC 2026):** Impella CP in LVEF ≤35% complex PCI gave **no benefit and higher CV death** (26.7% vs 14.5%).

- **PROTECT IV:** results pending.

Routine MCS for high-risk PCI is not supported.

### Mechanical complications of MI

- **Timing:** bimodal (<24 h and 3–5 d). Suspect with any new murmur or shock ≥3 d after MI, especially late presentation or first MI without reperfusion.

### Ventricular septal rupture

- Risk: older, female, anterior MI, no prior smoking, late presentation, higher Killip/HR.

- Anterior MI → apical VSR; inferior MI → basal VSR (worse, harder to repair).

- Harsh holosystolic murmur; **O₂ step-up RA→RV **.

- Highest shock mortality (~87% in SHOCK registry). Afterload reduction and IABP/Impella bridge (Impella risks shunt reversal/embolization); surgery. Device closure for residual defects or as a bridge.

### Papillary muscle rupture

- **Posteromedial muscle** (single supply from PDA; inferior MI).

- Abrupt pulmonary edema/shock; murmur may be soft or absent.

- **Giant v waves, no step-up.**

- Nitroprusside (if BP allows), IABP/Impella, emergent surgery.

- Papillary muscle _dysfunction_ gives slower, milder MR.

### Free-wall rupture

- Older women, HTN, first MI, late lysis.

- Sudden tamponade, PEA, equalized diastolic pressures. Pericardiocentesis only as a temporizing bridge to surgery. LV pseudoaneurysm (narrow neck) → surgery.

### RV infarct

   - Hypotension, clear lungs, ↑ JVP; RAP/PCWP >0.8. Maintain preload (avoid nitrates/diuretics), AV synchrony, inotropes, RV MCS.

- **Bezold–Jarisch reflex:** bradycardia/hypotension with inferior reperfusion; atropine and fluids.

- **Takotsubo with LVOTO:** avoid inotropes/nitrates. Fluids, BB, phenylephrine.

## 9. Complex PCI: Calcium, CTO, Bypass Grafts, ISR and Stent Thrombosis

### Calcium and plaque modification

- ~⅓ of PCI lesions have moderate–severe calcium (underestimated by angiography). Risk: CKD/ESRD, DM, age, smoking.

- Consequences: device-delivery failure, stent underexpansion/malapposition, polymer damage, dislodgement, perforation, ISR/ST.

- Goal of atherectomy is **modification** for balloon/stent expansion, not debulking to a final result.

- **Balloons**

   - Semi-compliant: deliverable, but “dog-bone” edge dissection.

   - Non-compliant: focused force; used for predilation of fibrocalcific lesions and post-dilation.

   - Cutting/scoring: ostial lesions, ISR, prevent slippage. Inflate slowly; do not exceed rated pressure (blade fracture/perforation).

   - Super-high-pressure balloon (OPN).

- **If pre-dilation fully expands the balloon **, you can stent without atherectomy.

### Rotational atherectomy

- Diamond-coated front of an elliptical burr. **Differential cutting** ablates inelastic tissue and deflects elastic wall. **Orthogonal displacement of friction** at >60k rpm.

- Debris <10 µm (smaller than capillaries), but a large burden still causes slow flow.

- **RotaWire:** 0.009" shaft with 0.014" radiopaque tip. Floppy (less bias) vs Extra Support (more bias, straightens vessel). Burr must not reach the radiopaque tip (fracture).

- **Burr sizing:** 1.25/1.5 mm (6F), 1.75 (7F), 2.0 (8F), up to 2.5 mm (9–10F). **Burr:artery ratio 0.5–0.6 **; >0.7 → perforation/complications (STRATAS, CARAT). Most cases use a 1.25–1.5 mm burr.

- **Technique:**

   - 135–180k rpm; favor lower (~140–160k).

   - **Avoid decelerations >5,000 rpm.**

   - Pecking motion; runs ≤15–20 s.

   - **Never stop the burr distal to the lesion.**

   - Rotaglide flush (egg/olive-oil allergy) ± verapamil/NTG in flush.

### Complications

   - Slow/no-reflow: small burr, low speed, short runs, vasodilators; treat downstream lesions that trap debris.

   - **Bradycardia/AV block** with dominant RCA/LCx: TVP, or atropine/aminophylline.

   - Perforation (oversized burr, angulation, wire bias). **Burr entrapment** (“Kokeshi,” usually small burr through a tight lesion):

      - Lock the advancer and pull the whole system while controlling the guide.

      - Second guide/wire with balloon dilation beside the burr. Cut the drive shaft, advance a guide extension over it for coaxial traction.

      - Surgery as last resort.

- **Trials:** ERBAC/COBRA/DART showed ↑ procedural success but no restenosis benefit. **ROTAXUS:** routine rota ↑ success, no MACE benefit (and more late loss). **PREPARE-CALC:** rota ≈ modified balloons.

**Orbital atherectomy**

   - Eccentric 1.25 mm diamond-coated crown, **coated on both sides** (sands forward and backward).

   - Orbit diameter ↑ with speed (80k “low” → 120k “high”), slower advancement and more passes.

   - **ViperWire:** 0.012" shaft, 0.014" tip. **ViperSlide:** soybean oil, egg-yolk phospholipids.

   - Runs ≤30 s; start low speed; ~1 mm/s advance.

   - **ORBIT II:** safe and effective (single arm). **ECLIPSE (2024):** routine OA vs balloon prep in severe calcium — no ↓ 1- yr TVF, no ↑ MSA.

- **Laser (ELCA) and intravascular lithotripsy**

   - **ELCA** is a 308 nm excimer laser over a standard 0.014" wire.

      - Mechanisms: **photochemical** (bond breakage), **photothermal** (vapor bubble), **photomechanical** (shock wave).

      - **Flush saline continuously; no contrast or blood** in the field (both absorb energy → dissection).

      - Advance ~0.5–1 mm/s; titrate fluence and rate. Uses: uncrossable lesions, **underexpanded stents** (especially with contrast), thrombus, SVG, CTO cap. Relative contraindication in unprotected LM. Laser (not rota) is approved for SVG and ISR.

   - **IVL (Shockwave C2+)**

      - 2.5–4.0 × 12 mm; 1:1 sizing; 4 atm → pulses → 6 atm. Circumferential and deep calcium fracture. May trigger ventricular capture (“shocktopics”).

Low perforation rate (DISRUPT CAD III).

Crossing profile is bulky and may need pre-dilation.

### Wire and device crossing toolbox

- **Wires:** tapered hydrophilic/polymer (microchannels), buddy wire, stiffer support wire, knuckle. Pull back the stiff segment if you see an accordion pseudolesion.

- **Support:** microcatheter (Corsair, Turnpike/Gold, Tornus), guide extension, anchor balloon, larger or more supportive guide.

- **Modification:** smaller balloon, “grenadoplasty” (rupture balloon in lesion), rota/laser/IVL, subintimal strategies.

### Chronic total occlusion

- **Definition:** TIMI 0 for ≥3 mo (probable if duration unknown).

- Present in ~15–20% of angiograms; PAD is the strongest predictor. Non-IRA CTO in STEMI ↑ mortality (worst with shock).

- **Operator/center volume** matters for success and complications. Current success ~85–90%. MACE ~2–3%; death ~0.5–1% (PROGRESS-CTO, OPEN-CTO).

- **J-CTO** (1 point each: blunt cap, calcification, bend >45°, length ≥20 mm, prior failed attempt): 0 easy; 1 intermediate; 2 difficult; ≥3 very difficult Higher scores → dissection/re-entry or retrograde approach

- **Hybrid algorithm** (dual injection is essential). Four questions: proximal cap ambiguity, lesion length (≥20 mm), distal target quality, interventional collaterals. Clear cap, short lesion → **antegrade wire escalation **. If subintimal: parallel wire, or move to ADR/retrograde. Long lesion, good distal target → **antegrade dissection re-entry** (knuckle wire, CrossBoss, Stingray LP; STRAW to decompress hematoma).

   - Ambiguous cap, poor distal vessel, or prior failure with good collaterals → **retrograde** (septal > epicardial; reverse CART).

- **Stop the procedure if:** air kerma >5 Gy (strongly consider at >7 Gy); contrast >3.7× eGFR; >3 h; or remaining options are too risky. Consider an “investment procedure” (subintimal plaque modification, re-attempt ~2–3 mo).

- **Complications:** perforation (epicardial collaterals especially dangerous; **post-CABG loculated tamponade** ), donor-vessel injury/ischemia, septal hematoma (usually benign, rarely serious).

- **Evidence:**

**EuroCTO (2018):** ↑ angina and QoL vs OMT.

**DECISION-CTO (2019):** no MACE difference; high crossover.

**EXPLORE** (non-IRA CTO after STEMI) and **REVASC:** no LVEF improvement. No RCT survival benefit. **ACC/AHA 2021:** CTO PCI for refractory angina after treating non-CTO lesions: **2b **.

**Saphenous vein grafts**

**Failure modes:**

- Early (<1 mo): thrombosis/technical, ~10–15%

- 1 mo–1 yr: intimal hyperplasia

   - >1 yr: accelerated atherosclerosis with friable lipidrich, thin-capped plaque

- **Patency:** ~25% of SVGs failed at 12–18 mo (PREVENT IV); ~50% occluded by 10 yr. LIMA-LAD patency >90% at 10 yr; late LIMA failure from competitive flow, subclavian stenosis or anastomotic issues.

**VEST** (external stent): no patency improvement.

**Strategy:**

   - Prefer **native-vessel PCI** over SVG PCI when feasible (2a).

   - Redo CABG if failed or unused LIMA, multiple graft failures, heart team.

   - Radial access is reasonable for graft angiography (more contrast/crossover, fewer vascular complications).

- **SVG PCI risks:** distal embolization and no-reflow (10– 20%); periprocedural MI (predicts late death). Risk ↑ with plaque volume, degeneration score, female sex, CKD.

- **Embolic protection** (FilterWire EZ, Spider): needs a ~2.5– 3 cm distal landing zone; can clog; not possible for distalanastomosis lesions. **2021 guideline: 2a** (was Class 1). **GPI: no benefit (Class 3). Covered stents: no benefit.**

- No PCI of chronically occluded SVGs (Class 3).

- No-reflow prevention without EPD: IC nicardipine 100– 200 mcg, verapamil 100–250 mcg, adenosine, nitroprusside; direct stenting; undersized stents.

- **DES vs BMS in SVG:** ISAR-CABG ↓ TLR with DES; DIVA and BASKET-SAVAGE mixed. DES is generally used; any benefit wanes over time.

- **LIMA PCI:** rare (2–3%/yr failure). Challenges: ischemia, visualization, damping, wire pseudolesions, guide dissection. Engage with IMA or JR4 catheter.

### In-stent restenosis

**BMS ISR** is diffuse neointimal hyperplasia (SMC-rich, collagen III), peaking at 6–8 mo. BMS ISR rate ~20–30%. **DES ISR** is focal/edge, proteoglycan-rich, with late catchup, frequent peri-strut inflammation and **neoatherosclerosis **. Endothelialization can take years. Current DES ISR ~1–2%/yr.

~⅓ of ISR presents as ACS.

**Definition:** ≥50% DS within the stent or 5 mm edges (symptoms/ischemia), or ≥70% DS; IVUS MLA <4 mm² (<6 LM).

**Risk factors:**

Patient: DM (insulin resistance blunts mTOR-inhibitor effect), CKD/HD, smoking, female sex, ACS. Lesion: small vessel, long lesion, bifurcation, ostial, calcified, CTO, SVG.

Procedure: underexpansion (strongest), geographic miss, residual edge plaque, stent gap, fracture. Device: thick struts, polymer/drug hypersensitivity.

**Management:**

**Image first** to define the mechanism.

- Underexpansion → high-pressure NC, IVL, or laser for calcium behind struts.

- Neointima/neoatherosclerosis → **DES** (Class 1) or **DCB **. The **AGENT paclitaxel DCB is FDA-approved (2024) **; AGENT IDE showed ↓ TLF vs POBA.

- Cutting/scoring balloon to prevent slippage (2b). Recurrent multi-layer DES-ISR → **intravascular brachytherapy **, DCB, or CABG. Avoid a third layer. Laser > POBA for debulking ISR. Rota for ISR is mixed (ROSTER favorable, ARTIST unfavorable).

### Stent thrombosis

**ARC definitions:**

**Definite:** angiographic or pathologic confirmation.

- **Probable:** unexplained death ≤30 d, or target-territory MI without angiography.

**Possible:** unexplained death >30 d.

- **Timing:** acute <24 h; subacute 1–30 d; late 30 d–1 yr; very late >1 yr.

- **Rates:** <1% at 30 d, then ~0.2–0.6%/yr. Mostly early. Presents as STEMI/SCD; 30-d mortality ~10–25%.

- **Early ST** is procedural: underexpansion/small MSA, edge dissection, geographic miss, tissue protrusion, inflow/outflow disease, inadequate antithrombotic therapy (e.g., after stopping bivalirudin).

- **Late/very late ST:** premature DAPT discontinuation (strongest early predictor), late-acquired malapposition, neoatherosclerosis, fracture, hypersensitivity. Patient factors: ACS, DM, CKD, low EF, long total stent length.

- After 6 mo on current DES, the ST risk from DAPT interruption is low. **Treatment:**

   - Primary PCI; aspiration only if large thrombus.

   - **Intravascular imaging** to find the mechanism; stent again only for dissection, underexpansion not fixed by balloon, or new disease.

   - Switch or reload to ticagrelor/prasugrel; GPI; check adherence.

   - Consider genotyping (CYP2C19 LOF) and prolonged DAPT.

- Ticagrelor, prasugrel and cangrelor have lower ST rates than clopidogrel.

- **DAPT score:** age ≥75 −2, 65–74 −1, <65 0. +1 each for smoking, DM, MI at presentation, prior PCI/MI, stent <3 mm, paclitaxel stent. +2 for CHF/LVEF <30% or SVG PCI. **≥2 favors >12 mo DAPT.**

## 10. Management of PCI Complications

### Coronary perforation

- **Epidemiology:** ~0.3–0.5% overall (higher in CTO and atherectomy). Associated with tamponade, MI, emergency surgery and death. **Ellis classification:**

   - **I:** extraluminal crater without extravasation

   - **II:** pericardial/myocardial blush without a jet **III:** jet ≥1 mm

   - **III-CS:** cavity spilling into a chamber (usually benign)

- **Immediate steps (all types):**

   - **Proximal balloon tamponade** first; keep wire position. Support hemodynamics.

   - Stat echo (IC agitated contrast/Definity can localize an ongoing leak). Pericardiocentesis if tamponade (echo or fluoro guided; autotransfusion). Notify surgery early. No further anticoagulant/GPI.

- **Large-vessel perforation** → **covered stent** (PK Papyrus, 5–6F; Graftmaster). Use a **dual-guide “block-anddeliver”** (ping-pong): the second guide delivers the covered stent while the first keeps the balloon up, minimizing un-tamponaded time. Residual leak → look for geographic miss.

- **Distal wire perforation** (polymer-jacketed or stiff wires): often recognized late, leading to delayed tamponade. Balloon occlusion.

   - **Embolize via microcatheter:** coils (detachable allow repositioning), autologous fat or subcutaneous tissue (mix with contrast), thrombin, microspheres. Aspirate through the microcatheter to collapse the branch.

- Covered stent across the branch origin as a last resort.

- **Collateral perforation** (CTO): embolize **from both sides **. Epicardial collateral perforation is more dangerous than septal.

- **Protamine** is not routine. Use it (partial) only for a persistent leak after tamponade, after all equipment is out. Avoid after a covered stent if sealed (thrombosis). It does not reverse bivalirudin.

- **Prior CABG:** loculated, often posterior effusions that cannot be tapped percutaneously → CT-guided drainage or surgery. Mortality is high. SVG/LIMA perforation is catastrophic.

- **After a covered stent:** higher ST and restenosis; longer DAPT.

- **Myocardial staining** (end-hole injection or pigtail entrapment) is usually benign; large stains can cause VT. **RV perforation/free-wall rupture** is the dreaded RVventriculography complication.

- **Intramyocardial hematoma or deep-wall dissection** (e.g., bridged LAD, post-dilation) is usually stable. Diagnosis of exclusion; echo.

### No-reflow / slow flow

- **Definition:** TIMI <3 (or TIMI 3 with myocardial blush grade 0–1) without epicardial obstruction.

- **Mechanisms:** distal embolization; ischemia-reperfusion (neutrophils, ROS, endothelial swelling, edema); vasospasm; individual susceptibility.

- **Settings:** STEMI (high thrombus, >6 h ischemia), SVG, atherectomy, long lesions.

- **Risk factors:** older age, DM, CKD, HF/Killip >2, large infarct, long ischemic time, lesion >15 mm.

- **Prognosis:** ~2× long-term mortality.

- **Algorithm:**

   - Exclude dissection, thrombus, air and spasm (IC NTG). Aspirate thrombus if present.

   - **Deliver vasodilators distally** through a microcatheter or aspiration catheter, not the guide.

- **IC doses (distal):**

   - Adenosine 30–60 mcg boluses (up to 1–2 mg); AV block in RCA; endothelium-independent.

   - Nitroprusside 50–200 mcg (hypotension).

   - Verapamil 100–250 mcg (up to 1 mg; bradycardia). Nicardipine 50–200 mcg.

   - Epinephrine 50–200 mcg (useful with hypotension/bradycardia; causes tachycardia).

- Choose the agent by HR and BP: bradycardic/hypotensive → epinephrine; tachycardic/hypertensive → nitroprusside or CCB.

### NTG does not treat microvascular obstruction.

- GPI can be a bailout for thrombus; no proof it treats noreflow.

**Prevention:** direct stenting; avoid aggressive postdilation in thrombotic lesions; EPD in SVG; small burrs, low speed and short runs; pretreatment with nicardipine/verapamil (not adenosine) in small studies; high-intensity statin.

- **“Epicardial no-reflow”** during atherectomy: debris trapped at a tight downstream lesion. Flush; if no improvement, balloon the distal lesion (do not stent the proximal segment yet).

### Dissection and abrupt closure

- **NHLBI types:** A–B minor; C (extraluminal cap); D (spiral); E (filling defect); F (occlusion). Types C–F need stenting. Abrupt closure is mostly a POBA-era problem (dissection + thrombus). It is often transient, but stent it.

- **Catheter-induced ostial dissection** (classically Amplatz in the RCA; guide extensions): presents with ST elevation, VF or collapse.

   - Stop contrast injections.

   - Keep or obtain distal wire position in the true lumen (confirm with IVUS).

   - Stent the ostium/entry point promptly to seal and stop antegrade and retrograde propagation, then cover distal extension.

   - Check the aortic root for retrograde extension. Limited extension (<40 mm) usually seals once the ostium is sealed; extensive → CT/TEE, surgery.

- Coronary dissection with a damped guide pressure trace: never inject; pull back.

### Air embolism

- **Presentation:** abrupt cutoff or slow flow in one or more vessels without a dissection plane; chest pain, ST elevation, VF, hypotension.

- **Causes:** inadequate aspiration/flushing during exchanges, loose connections, balloon rupture, large devices in small guides.

**Treatment:**

100% O₂.

- Forceful saline injection or aspiration through a catheter; disrupt bubbles with a wire or balloon. Vasopressors or CPR if needed.

Most resolve.

**Prevention:** meticulous flushing and back-bleeding; wet connections.

### Equipment loss / stent dislodgement

**Risk:** calcium, proximal tortuosity, direct stenting without predilation, non-coaxial guide on withdrawal (most common), previously deployed damaged stents catching new ones.

- 70–90% of lost devices are retrievable.

- **If the wire is still through the stent:** advance a small (1.5 mm) balloon beyond or into the stent, inflate at low pressure, and withdraw the balloon-stent-guide as a unit. **Other options:** twist two wires to entangle; snare (gooseneck/multi-loop, may need a larger guide or 6F catheter as a “mother” sheath); biopsy forceps or basket. **If unretrievable: crush against the wall** with another stent, or deploy in place if feasible. Leave small wire fragments in distal branches.

- A 300 cm wire doubled in a 4–5F catheter works as a makeshift snare.

### Other intraprocedural events

- **Vagal reaction vs RP bleed:** both hypotensive. Vagal = bradycardia, responds to atropine/fluids. RP = tachycardia (bradycardia possible), transient response to fluids only.

- **Acute stent thrombosis** after bivalirudin stops or with no P2Y12 load: re-anticoagulate, reload with ticagrelor/prasugrel, GPI/cangrelor.

- **Atrial septal occluder embolization** (0.5–1%; usually immediate):

   - Risk: deficient rims, undersizing, floppy septum, excessive tension.

   - Retrieve with snare/forceps into a sheath ≥2F larger than delivery (bevel the tip).

   - Move the device to the PA, aorta or IVC before manipulating; ventricular location often needs surgery. CXR/TTE before discharge.

## 11. Peripheral Artery, Renal, Subclavian and Carotid Disease

### PAD epidemiology and presentation

- **Prevalence:** ~7% at age 40, ~20% at >80. Most are asymptomatic; ~⅓ have typical claudication; <5% have CLTI.

- **Polyvascular disease** (CAD/CVD) in ~40%. 5-yr mortality 15–30% (~75% CV).

- **Risk factors:** smoking > DM > dyslipidemia > HTN.

   - Smoking favors aorto-iliac disease.

   - DM favors femoropopliteal/tibial disease.

   - Young smoker with distal disease → Buerger’s.

- **At-risk groups:** ≥65 y; 50–64 y with risk factors; <50 with DM + 1 other risk factor; known atherosclerosis elsewhere.

- **Natural history** (5 yr):

   - Claudication: 70–80% stable, 10–20% worsen, 1–2% progress to CLTI.

   - **CLTI at 1 yr:** ~25% amputation, ~25% dead.

- Major amputation: BKA 5–8% perioperative mortality (AKA higher); ~50% of BKA patients regain ambulation.

- **Classification:** Rutherford 0–6 (4 = rest pain, 5 = minor tissue loss, 6 = major tissue loss). CLTI = rest pain, nonhealing wound or gangrene ≥2 wk; stage with WIfI.

**Claudication differential:**

- Neurogenic (spinal stenosis): worse standing, relieved by sitting or flexion (“shopping-cart sign”). Venous: heaviness, relieved by elevation. Chronic compartment syndrome (athletes); hip/knee arthritis; Baker cyst.

**Ulcers:**

- **Arterial:** distal/toes, between digits, punched-out, dry pale base, painful.

- **Venous:** medial malleolus, shallow, exudative, hemosiderin, lipodermatosclerosis.

- **Neuropathic:** plantar pressure points, callus, insensate.

**CLTI rest pain** is nocturnal and relieved by dependency (sleeping in a chair).

### Testing

**Resting ABI** is first-line (higher ankle pressure of that leg / higher arm pressure):

- **≤0.90 abnormal**

- 0.91–0.99 borderline

- 1.00–1.40 normal

- **>1.40 non-compressible** → TBI (≤0.70 abnormal)

- **Exercise ABI** if resting ABI is normal/borderline with symptoms. Abnormal = ankle pressure ↓ >30 mmHg or ABI ↓ >20%. ~⅓ with normal resting ABI become abnormal.

- **Segmental pressures:** ≥20 mmHg drop between levels localizes stenosis.

- **Doppler waveforms:** triphasic (normal) → biphasic → monophasic (severe). PVR loses the dicrotic notch, then amplitude.

- **Wound healing:** toe pressure ≥30 mmHg and TcPO₂ ≥30– 40 mmHg favor healing; <20–30 predicts poor healing. ABI can be falsely normal in CLTI (calcified tibials). **Anatomic imaging** (duplex, CTA, MRA) only when revascularization is being considered. Invasive angiography only with intent to treat.

### Medical therapy (2024 ACC/AHA PAD guideline)

- **Smoking cessation** (Class 1).

- **Supervised exercise therapy** (Class 1): ≥30–45 min, ≥3×/wk, ≥12 wk; structured community programs also OK.

- **High-intensity statin** for ≥50% LDL ↓; add ezetimibe/PCSK9i if LDL ≥70.

- ACEi/ARB; BP control.

**Antithrombotics:**

- SAPT (aspirin or clopidogrel; CAPRIE favored clopidogrel) for symptomatic PAD.

- **Rivaroxaban 2.5 mg BID + aspirin** (COMPASS; VOYAGER PAD after revascularization) ↓ MACE and MALE (Class 1).

Short DAPT after endovascular therapy is reasonable. No full-dose OAC without another indication (WAVE). Ticagrelor = clopidogrel (EUCLID). Vorapaxar ↓ ALI (not after stroke).

- **DM:** SGLT2i and GLP-1 RA ↓ MACE. **STRIDE (2025):** semaglutide ↑ walking distance in PAD + T2DM.

- **Cilostazol** 100 mg BID (PDE3): ↑ walking distance; **contraindicated in HF **.

- **Pentoxifylline and chelation: not recommended.**

**UPDATED** The original said “statin to LDL <100” and “ASA alone for most.” Current targets are ≥50% LDL reduction with add-on therapy if LDL ≥70, and low-dose rivaroxaban + ASA for symptomatic PAD.

### Revascularization

- **Asymptomatic PAD:** never revascularize to prevent progression.

- **Claudication:** only if lifestyle-limiting after GDMT and supervised exercise. Exercise first, even for Rutherford 3. **CLTI:** revascularize (Class 1) with a multidisciplinary team. Aim for **in-line flow to the wound angiosome **, revascularize early (heel wounds), close follow-up. Wet gangrene → debridement first.

- **BEST-CLI (2022):** surgical bypass with adequate singlesegment GSV ↓ MALE/death vs endovascular-first. **BASIL-2 (2023):** for infrapopliteal disease, vein bypassfirst ↑ amputation/death vs endovascular-first.

**Anatomy:**

   - Endovascular is best for iliac and focal femoropopliteal lesions (<10–15 cm).

   - Long, complex aorto-iliac, CFA/profunda disease → surgery (CFA endarterectomy).

   - Treat inflow before outflow.

   - Infrapopliteal intervention for claudication is not durable; avoid.

- **Paclitaxel devices:** FDA (2023) found no excess mortality signal on updated data.

- **Popliteal aneurysm ≥2 cm → repair.**

### Acute limb ischemia (symptoms <2 weeks)

- **6 P’s:** pain, pallor, pulselessness, poikilothermia, paresthesia, paralysis.

- **Etiology:** embolic (AF, LV thrombus, aneurysm, paradoxical; lodges at CFA or popliteal bifurcation) vs thrombotic (native PAD, stent or graft thrombosis; APS, JAK2, HIT, malignancy).

- **Immediate IV heparin** (Class 1).

- **Revascularization:** catheter-directed thrombolysis or mechanical thrombectomy for I–IIa; surgery or mechanical options for IIb.

- Watch for **compartment syndrome** after reperfusion (fasciotomy).

- **Subacute ischemia** (weeks): long, easily crossed thrombotic occlusions. Use thrombolysis/thrombectomy and consider EPD (embolization risk).

### Subclavian stenosis

- Left ~4× more common than right; present in ~11% of PAD patients. Usually asymptomatic; **inter-arm SBP difference >15 mmHg **.

- **Screen before CABG with IMA:** arm BP difference, chest radiation, vasculitis, known PAD.

- **Treat for:** arm claudication, symptomatic vertebral steal, **coronary-subclavian steal** (LIMA angina), planned LIMA use, dialysis access. Endovascular first (>90% 3-yr patency).

### Renal artery disease

- **ASTRAL and CORAL:** no benefit of stenting atherosclerotic RAS for BP, renal function or events. **Routine stenting is not recommended.**

- **Consider stenting for:** recurrent flash pulmonary edema; rapidly declining renal function with bilateral/solitary kidney stenosis; truly resistant HTN on optimal meds. **Significance:** systolic gradient ≥20 mmHg, mean ≥10 mmHg, Pd/Pa ≤0.90, or renal FFR ≤0.80. **Clues:**

   - HTN onset <30 or >55 y; resistant, malignant or accelerating HTN.

   - Kidney size difference >1.5 cm or unexplained atrophy. **Flash pulmonary edema **; Cr ↑ >30% after ACEi/ARB. Unexplained CKD; multivessel CAD/PAD.

- **Physiology:**

   - **Unilateral RAS:** renin-dependent HTN. Contralateral kidney has pressure natriuresis (no volume overload). **ACEi lowers BP.**

   - **Bilateral RAS or solitary kidney:** volume-dependent; renin suppressed. **ACEi causes AKI (Cr rises)** with little BP effect.

- **Fibromuscular dysplasia:** young/middle-aged women, “string of beads” in mid/distal renal and ICA/vertebral arteries.

- **Balloon angioplasty without stent** for HTN (high cure rate).

One-time head-to-pelvis CTA/MRA (aneurysms, dissection); strong SCAD association.

**UPDATED** The original said Cr decreases with ACEi in bilateral RAS. Creatinine rises.

### Carotid disease

### Definitions and natural history

- **Symptomatic** = ipsilateral TIA, stroke or amaurosis fugax **within 6 months** (not dizziness or syncope). Stroke risk is highest in the first 2 wk.

- **Measurement:** NASCET % = (1 − residual lumen / normal distal ICA) × 100.

- **Duplex ≥70%:** ICA PSV >230 cm/s and ICA/CCA ratio >4. CTA/MRA confirm.

- **Carotid disease causes 10–20% of ischemic strokes **, mostly **embolic **. Revascularization prevents embolism, not hypoperfusion.

- Asymptomatic stenosis also marks systemic risk (strokes in other territories). Total occlusion: no revascularization.

- **Arch anatomy:**

   - Common origin of innominate and L CCA (“bovine,” ~15–25%).

   - L vertebral from the arch (~3–5%).

   - **Aberrant right subclavian** (arteria lusoria, ~1%): arises distal to L SCA, retroesophageal; makes right radial access hard.

Type III arch ↑ CAS embolic risk.

### Symptomatic stenosis

- **NASCET/ECST:** CEA benefit is greatest for 70–99% (large ARR), modest for 50–69%, harmful <30%.

- Revascularization for 70–99% is Class 1 (CEA if perioperative risk <6%). **Operating within 2 wk** of the event is reasonable (2a).

**CAS is an alternative **, especially for high surgical risk or anatomy. **CREST:** no difference in the composite; CAS had more minor strokes, CEA more MI. Age >70 favors CEA.

### Asymptomatic stenosis

- **ACAS/ACST** (pre-modern statins): modest CEA benefit, mostly in men <75.

- **SPACE-2:** no benefit of either revascularization strategy vs best medical therapy (underpowered). **ACST-2 (2021):** CAS ≈ CEA.

- **CREST-2 (NEJM 2025) **, ≥70% asymptomatic stenosis, credentialed operators:

**CAS + intensive medical management ↓ periprocedural stroke/death + 4-yr ipsilateral stroke (2.8% vs 6.0%, P=0.02; NNT ~31).**

- **CEA + intensive medical management: 3.7% vs 5.3% (P=0.24, NS).**

**CMS NCD (Oct 2023):** CAS covered for symptomatic ≥50% and asymptomatic ≥70% **regardless of surgical risk **. Requires shared decision-making, pre/post neurologic assessment and imaging confirmation. Facilitycertification requirement removed.

**UPDATED** The original said “no board scenario where we stent an asymptomatic patient” and “CMS only reimburses high surgical risk.” Both are outdated after CREST-2 and the 2023 NCD. Individual patient selection and life expectancy still matter.

### High surgical-risk features (favor CAS/TCAR)

- **Anatomic:** prior CEA with restenosis, neck radiation or radical dissection, lesion at/above C2 or below clavicle, contralateral occlusion, contralateral laryngeal nerve palsy, immobile neck, tracheostomy.

- **Physiologic:** age ≥80, NYHA III–IV, LVEF <30%, unstable angina or recent MI, LM/multivessel CAD needing surgery within 30 d, severe lung disease. Young patients generally do better with CEA. **TCAR** (flow reversal) is an alternative.

### CAS technique and complications

Plan with arch imaging (CTA). Embolic risk comes from arch manipulation, crossing, and **post-dilation (highestrisk step) **. Accept 10–20% residual stenosis.

**EPD required.** Distal filters fail with poor landing zone or ECA collaterals. Proximal occlusion (MoMA) works before crossing but needs collateral tolerance.

- **Visible thrombus → do not stent.**

- **Baroreceptor response** (bradycardia/hypotension): hold antihypertensives the morning of the procedure; atropine before post-dilation; phenylephrine/norepinephrine; midodrine or pseudoephedrine if persistent. Less common after prior CEA (denervated). **CEA-specific risks:** MI, cranial nerve injury, neck hematoma, early thrombosis.

**Hyperperfusion syndrome** (days after CAS/CEA): ipsilateral headache, seizures, focal deficits, ICH. Highest risk with tight stenosis, poor collaterals/contralateral occlusion, and HTN. Treat with **strict BP control** (labetalol, clonidine), ICU and imaging.

## 12. Structural Heart Disease

### Transcatheter aortic valve replacement

### Indications

- **Severe AS:** Vmax ≥4 m/s, MG ≥40 mmHg, AVA ≤1.0 cm². Symptom onset is the “cliff” for survival. **2020 ACC/AHA (current US VHD guideline) **, symptomatic severe AS:

   - Age <65 or life expectancy >20 yr → SAVR. 65–80 → shared decision, SAVR or TF-TAVR. >80 or life expectancy <10 yr → TF-TAVR. High or prohibitive surgical risk → TAVR.

### Asymptomatic severe AS

- **Class 1 AVR:** LVEF <50%; abnormal exercise test (↓ BP or symptoms); undergoing other cardiac surgery.

- **2a:** very severe (Vmax ≥5, MG ≥60), rapid progression (≥0.3 m/s/yr), BNP >3× normal.

**Newer evidence:**

- **EARLY TAVR (2024):** TF-TAVR in asymptomatic severe AS ↓ death/stroke/unplanned CV hospitalization vs surveillance. **FDA approved TAVR for asymptomatic**

**severe AS (May 2025).**

**AVATAR/EVOLVED:** early SAVR benefit.

- **ESC/EACTS 2025:** TAVI favored for **≥70 y** (was 75); intervention for asymptomatic severe high-gradient AS at low procedural risk.

**Low-risk RCTs:**

- **PARTNER 3:** TAVR ↓ 1-yr death/stroke/rehospitalization; composite similar at 5 yr.

**Evolut Low Risk:** noninferior.

   - **NOTION 10-yr:** similar mortality; less SVD with TAVR. **DEDICATE (2024):** TAVR noninferior/superior to SAVR at low–intermediate risk (≥65 y).

- **Bicuspid** anatomy was excluded from most RCTs; increasingly treated in selected anatomy.

- **STS risk:** low <4%, intermediate 4–8%, high >8%. **Frailty, porcelain aorta, hostile chest** (radiation, patent grafts) favor TAVR.

**UPDATED** The original said “if you can’t do TF, palliative care.” Alternative access (transcarotid, subclavian/axillary, transcaval) is used before palliation. TF remains best (~95% of cases).

### Devices (US)

- **SAPIEN 3/Ultra RESILIA** (balloon-expandable, intraannular): approved for native AS at all risk levels, aortic/mitral/pulmonic ViV, and asymptomatic severe AS. **Evolut FX+** (self-expanding, supra-annular): better hemodynamics in small annuli. **SMART (2024):** less bioprosthetic valve dysfunction than SAPIEN in small annuli; clinical outcomes similar.

- **Navitor** (Abbott, self-expanding intra-annular; successor to Portico): high/extreme risk.

- **JenaValve Trilogy: FDA-approved March 2026** for symptomatic severe **aortic regurgitation** at high surgical risk (ALIGN-AR). It is the first TAVR approved for AR; offlabel TAVR in AR otherwise embolizes (no calcium anchor).

### Pre-procedure CT

- Annulus sizing, LVOT calcium, iliofemoral access (minimal lumen diameter vs sheath), coronary heights, sinus dimensions.

- **Coronary obstruction risk (native):** coronary height <10– 12 mm, sinus of Valsalva <30 mm, bulky leaflet calcium, low sinotubular junction. Left main is involved more often than RCA.

- **ViV: valve-to-coronary distance (VTC) <4 mm high risk** (4–6 gray zone). Externally mounted leaflets (Mitroflow, Trifecta) are highest risk. Also check valve-to-STJ distance for sinus sequestration in TAV-in-TAV.

### CAD and TAVR

- >50% of TAVR patients have CAD. Coronary evaluation (CCTA or angiography) before TAVR is Class 1.

- **ACTIVATION (2021):** routine pre-TAVR PCI did not meet noninferiority; ↑ bleeding.

- **NOTION-3 (2024):** PCI of ≥1 lesion with FFR ≤0.80 or DS ≥90% (proximal, ≥2.5 mm) ↓ death/MI/urgent revascularization at 2 yr vs conservative care; ↑ bleeding.

- 2020 guideline: PCI reasonable for ≥70% proximal disease (2a). Complex multivessel disease (SYNTAX >32) favors SAVR + CABG.

- **Coronary access after TAVR** is harder with supra-annular SE frames (use JL3.5/JR4 through a frame cell ± guide extension) than with short BE frames (JL/EBU). Commissural alignment helps.

**UPDATED** The original said “PCI before TAVR only for symptomatic proximal lesions since PCI doesn’t improve outcomes.” NOTION-3 supports treating significant CAD.

**Conduction disturbances**

- **PPM rates:** ~5–10% with BE valves, ~15–25% with SE valves (higher in real-world practice than in trials). New LBBB ~20%.

- **Predictors: baseline RBBB** (strongest), deep implant, short membranous septum, LVOT/device-landing-zone calcium, oversizing, SE valve, intraprocedural transient CHB.

- **Cusp-overlap view** (overlapping RCC and LCC isolates the NCC, elongates the LVOT, removes parallax) allows a higher implant and ↓ PPM.

**New LBBB or PR/QRS prolongation:** monitor 24–48 h, ambulatory ECG, or EP study (HV interval). Progressive changes or transient CHB → PPM.

**Stroke, antithrombotics, leaflet thrombosis, durability**

- **Stroke:** ~2–3% at 30 d, mostly periprocedural. New MRI lesions in >80%.

- **Cerebral embolic protection** (Sentinel): captures debris in ~99% but **PROTECTED TAVR (2022)** and **BHF PROTECTTAVI (2025)** showed no stroke reduction. Not routine. **Antithrombotics:**

   - **POPular TAVI:** aspirin alone bleeds less than aspirin + clopidogrel; OAC alone bleeds less than OAC + clopidogrel.

   - **GALILEO:** rivaroxaban without an OAC indication ↑ death/bleeding. **ATLANTIS:** apixaban not superior. **ENVISAGE:** edoxaban noninferior to VKA in AF, with more GI bleeding.

   - **Default is lifelong low-dose aspirin **; OAC only if otherwise indicated. No routine DAPT.

- **HALT/subclinical leaflet thrombosis** (CT: HALT/HAM): common, reversible with OAC, weakly linked to stroke. Not the same as clinical valve thrombosis (↑ gradients, which is treated with OAC). No routine CT screening or empiric OAC.

**Durability:** NOTION 10-yr, PARTNER 2/3, Evolut data show TAVR SVD ≤ SAVR through 5–10 yr. Younger patients have earlier failure; plan lifetime management (TAV-inSAV favorable; TAV-in-TAV needs leaflet-overhang and commissural planning).

### Valve-in-valve

Good option for failed surgical valves at high redo risk. **Does not fix prosthesis–patient mismatch** (the small label size persists), so consider bioprosthetic valve fracture.

- **BVF:** high-pressure NC balloon fractures fracturable rings (e.g., Magna/Magna Ease, Mitroflow; not Hancock II or Trifecta) and ↓ gradient.

- Higher implant ↓ gradient but ↑ coronary risk. **BASILICA** (electrosurgical leaflet laceration) or chimney/snorkel stenting for coronary obstruction risk.

**TAVR vs SAVR complication profile**

**SAVR:** more AF, AKI, bleeding, longer length of stay. **TAVR:** more PVL (≥moderate ↑ mortality), PPM/LBBB, vascular complications.

### TAVR complications

- **Coronary obstruction** (~0.7% native, higher in ViV) Presents as hypotension, ST changes or VF right after deployment; can be delayed.

   - Mortality ~40–50%.

   - Prevention in high-risk anatomy: wire and undeployed stent in the coronary (“chimney”), BASILICA, recapturable valve.

   - Treatment: PCI (chimney), hemodynamic support, surgery.

After chimney stenting, a flapping lucency or slow flow means deploy the parked stent.

### Annular rupture

Risk: BE > SE; oversizing >20% area; LVOT/annular calcium; small annulus; aggressive post-dilation. Presents as sudden hypotension/tamponade.

- Pericardiocentesis, reverse heparin, surgery; small contained ruptures may be managed conservatively.

### Embolization/malposition

Risk: loss of pacing capture during BE deployment, undersizing, high implant, bulging septum, low calcium (AR), large annulus, male/large BSA.

SE valves: recapture or snare.

- **Keep the wire through the valve.** Pull an embolized BE valve with a partially inflated balloon into the aorta and deploy in a safe zone (descending aorta or distal to the great vessels), then implant a second valve. Ventricular embolization usually needs surgery. Associated with 30-d mortality.

**Tamponade:** LV wire perforation (stiff wire, delivery tip), RV pacing lead, aortic root rupture. Diagnose with echo and aortic root/LV angiography.

Vascular injury, aortic dissection, severe PVL (post-dilate, second valve, plug), acute AR, MR from wire entanglement.

### Mitral valve

### Mitral regurgitation

- **Primary (degenerative) vs secondary (ventricular or atrial functional).**

**Surgery for severe primary MR:**

- Symptomatic (Class 1).

- Asymptomatic with LVEF ≤60% or LVESD ≥40 mm (Class 1).

- Asymptomatic repair reasonable with durable-repair likelihood >95% and mortality <1%, or with progressive LV dilation, new AF or PASP >50.

**TEER for primary MR:**

   - **EVEREST II:** surgery more effective (more residual MR and later surgery with TEER) but TEER safer; symptoms similar at 5 yr.

   - Indicated for severe symptomatic primary MR at high or prohibitive surgical risk with favorable anatomy (2a).

   - **CLASP IID:** PASCAL noninferior to MitraClip.

- **TEER for secondary MR:**

   - **COAPT:** MitraClip + GDMT ↓ HF hospitalization and all-cause death (NNT ~6 for HFH at 2 yr) vs GDMT. Criteria: LVEF 20–50%, LVESD ≤70 mm, PASP ≤70, persistent severe MR on maximal GDMT/CRT.

   - **MITRA-FR:** neutral. Explained by “disproportionate” vs “proportionate” MR relative to LV size, lower MR severity, larger LVs and more residual MR.

   - **RESHAPE-HF2 (2024):** TEER ↓ HFH/CV death in moderate-to-severe SMR.

   - **MATTERHORN (2024):** TEER noninferior to surgery for SMR.

**ESC 2025:** Class I A in eligible SMR. ACC/AHA 2020: 2a. **Unfavorable TEER anatomy:** MVA <4 cm² or baseline MG >5 mmHg (stenosis), heavily calcified grasping zone, short posterior leaflet (<7 mm), Barlow disease with multiple segments, cleft, wide flail gap/width, rheumatic disease. **TMVR:**

**Tendyne** (transapical; FDA 2025).

**SAPIEN M3** ( **transseptal; FDA Dec 2025 **, ENCIRCLE): symptomatic MR not suitable for surgery or TEER, and MAC-related disease.

Main risk is **LVOT obstruction** (neo-LVOT <1.7–2.0 cm²); LAMPOON or alcohol septal ablation to mitigate. SAPIEN 3 approved for mitral ViV. Valve-in-ring and valve-in-MAC carry higher risk (MITRAL trial).

### Mitral stenosis

- **Rheumatic:** commissural fusion, leaflet and subvalvular thickening; annulus spared; younger patients. AF markedly worsens prognosis.

- **Degenerative (MAC):** annular calcium, elderly, high surgical risk; **not amenable to balloon valvotomy **. **PMBC for rheumatic MS** (Class 1): symptomatic, MVA ≤1.5 cm², favorable morphology, no LA thrombus, less than moderate MR.

   - **Wilkins score** (mobility, thickening, calcification, subvalvular; 1–4 each): **≤8 favorable **. Commissural calcium is unfavorable.

   - Success: MVA ≥1.5 cm² (or ≥50% increase) with ≤2+ MR.

   - Complications: severe MR (new giant v wave), tamponade, embolism, residual iatrogenic ASD.

- **Anticoagulation:** VKA for rheumatic MS with AF, prior embolism or LA thrombus (INVICTUS: rivaroxaban inferior).

- In rheumatic MS, thrombus is often in the LA body (not just the appendage), so LAAO is inappropriate.

- **Hemodynamics:** PCWP–LVEDP gradient (PCWP overestimates; transseptal LA pressure is best). Hypotension with low LV filling despite high right-sided pressures.

### Tricuspid, left atrial appendage, HCM

### Tricuspid regurgitation

**Mechanisms:**

- Ventricular functional (left heart disease/PH, RV dilation, leaflet tethering).

- Atrial functional (AF, annular dilation with preserved RV).

### CIED lead-related.

Primary (endocarditis, carcinoid, Ebstein).

**2020 ACC/AHA surgery:**

- Severe TR at time of left-sided surgery (Class 1). Progressive TR with annulus >40 mm (21 mm/m²) or prior RHF at left-sided surgery (2a). Isolated severe TR with RHF, before severe RV dysfunction (2a).

**Transcatheter:**

- **TriClip T-TEER** (FDA Apr 2024; TRILUMINATE: ↑ QoL, no ↓ death/HFH at 1 yr).

- **EVOQUE TTVR** (FDA Feb 2024; TRISCEND II: ↑ QoL/function; PPM ~15–25%; bleeding). **ESC 2025 (IIa):** symptomatic high-risk patients without severe RV dysfunction or pre-capillary PH.

### Left atrial appendage occlusion

- ~90% of non-valvular AF thrombi form in the LAA. Only ~50% of eligible patients receive OAC, often underdosed. **Trials:**

   - **PROTECT AF/PREVAIL** (Watchman vs warfarin), 5-yr pooled: noninferior composite; ↓ hemorrhagic stroke, disabling stroke and CV death; numerically more ischemic stroke.

   - **PRAGUE-17:** LAAO noninferior to DOAC (net composite; ↓ non-procedural bleeding at 4 yr). **Amulet IDE:** Amulet noninferior to Watchman for safety and efficacy, superior closure; more early procedural complications (effusion, embolization). **OPTION (2024):** after AF ablation, LAAO vs OAC ↓ non-procedural bleeding; noninferior for death/stroke/embolism.

   - **CHAMPION-AF (2026)** (Watchman FLX vs DOAC, OACeligible): noninferior for CV death/stroke/SE (5.7% vs 4.8%); ↓ non-procedural bleeding (10.9% vs 19.0%); **ischemic stroke/SE numerically higher** (3.2% vs 2.2%). **CLOSURE-AF (2026)** (HBR or OAC-contraindicated patients vs physician-directed best medical care, mostly DOAC): **LAAO failed noninferiority** for the net composite.

- **Guideline (2023 ACC/AHA/HRS AF):** LAAO **2a** for moderate–high stroke risk with a contraindication to long-term OAC from a non-reversible cause; **2b** for high bleeding risk or patient preference.

- **FDA label:** CHA₂DS₂-VASc high enough for OAC with an appropriate rationale to seek a non-drug alternative. **Post-Watchman FLX regimen:** OAC + aspirin for 45 d (or DAPT from the start), imaging (TEE/CT), DAPT to 6 mo, then aspirin. Amulet: DAPT.

- **Complications:** pericardial effusion needing intervention ~0.5–1%, device embolization <0.5%, procedural stroke. ICE-guided procedures avoid TEE and general anesthesia. - **Device-related thrombus (2–4%):** ↑ stroke 3–5×. Can occur anytime (mostly early). Risk: hypercoagulable state, pericardial effusion, CKD, **deep implant (>10 mm below the pulmonary ridge) **, permanent AF, low EF, large LAA.

Treat with OAC; recurrence common.

- **Peri-device leak: any leak, even <5 mm, ↑ ischemic events** (NCDR). Options: plugs, coils, second device, RF ablation.

**HCM and alcohol septal ablation**

**Hemodynamics:**

- **Brockenbrough–Braunwald–Morrow:** post-PVC ↑ LV– Ao gradient with **↓ aortic pulse pressure** (in AS the pulse pressure rises).

Spike-and-dome aortic waveform.

- Gradient ↑ with Valsalva, standing, amyl nitrite, isoproterenol; ↓ with phenylephrine, squatting, handgrip.

SAM-related MR (posteriorly directed) improves with phenylephrine. Anterior or central MR suggests intrinsic MV disease; get TEE.

- **Obstruction thresholds:** gradient ≥30 mmHg; **≥50 mmHg** (rest or provoked) with drug-refractory symptoms is the SRT threshold.

**2024 AHA/ACC HCM guideline:**

- Non-vasodilating BB first, then non-DHP CCB (avoid verapamil with severe gradients or hypotension). If still symptomatic: **myosin inhibitor** (mavacamten; **aficamten FDA Dec 2025 **; REMS/echo monitoring), **disopyramide **, or **SRT** at an experienced center (Class 1).

- VALOR-HCM: mavacamten ↓ SRT eligibility. MAPLEHCM: aficamten > metoprolol.

- Stop vasodilators and high-dose diuretics.

**Myectomy vs ASA:**

- **Myectomy preferred** with concomitant surgical disease (intrinsic MV or papillary muscle anomalies, CAD, other valve disease), **septum >30 mm **, and in young patients.

- **ASA** if surgery is contraindicated or high risk, or by informed patient preference.

- Center mortality <1% for both; similar long-term survival. Myectomy gives more complete gradient relief and fewer repeat procedures. SRT is for symptoms, not mortality.

**ASA technique:**

- TVP; wire and OTW balloon in the target septal (1st septal may arise from ramus/diagonal).

- Selective angiography to exclude leak into LAD/collaterals.

- **Myocardial contrast echo** confirms the basal septum at SAM contact and excludes papillary muscles/RV. 1–3 mL ethanol (~1 mL per 10 mm septal thickness); LAO-cranial view.

- Gradient falls over weeks to months with remodeling (acute drop from stunning).

**Conduction:**

### ASA → RBBB; myectomy → LBBB.

- CHB needing PPM ~10–15% after ASA, mostly within 24–48 h (monitor ≥48 h).

Baseline LBBB or 1st-degree AVB markedly ↑ CHB risk; favor myectomy or plan PPM/ICD. Best results with septum 15–30 mm.

### Congenital and shunt interventions

### Atrial septal defect

**Device closure only for secundum defects.** Primum, sinus venosus (associated partial anomalous pulmonary venous return) and coronary sinus defects need surgery.

- **Device requirements:** rims ≥5 mm (deficient aortic rim is common, OK, but ↑ erosion); defect ≤38 mm. **Measure Qp:Qs** and PVR.

- **Closure indications:** RA/RV enlargement or Qp:Qs ≥1.5 with net left-to-right shunt and no cyanosis.

   - **2025 ACHD:** PVR ≤2 WU → closure; 2–<5 WU → consider in expert centers; high PVR/Eisenmenger → no closure.

   - Small ASD (<1 cm, Qp:Qs <1.5, no RV enlargement) → no closure.

- **LV diastolic dysfunction** (older, HFpEF): test balloon occlusion. Marked LAP rise → fenestrated device or no closure (the ASD is a pop-off).

- **Complications:**

   - AF (early ~3%, late more)

   - Air embolism (keep hub low; hydrate)

   - Embolization

   - **Erosion** (Amplatzer ~0.1–0.3%; oversizing, deficient aortic rim)

   - Tamponade, device thrombus

- **After closure:** aspirin ± clopidogrel and endocarditis prophylaxis for 6 mo.

- Unrepaired ASD long term: RHF, atrial arrhythmias, PH, paradoxical embolism.

### Patent foramen ovale

- **RESPECT, CLOSE, REDUCE, DEFENSE-PFO:** closure ↓ recurrent stroke in **age 18–60 with embolic-appearing (cryptogenic) stroke** after full work-up (MRI, vessel imaging, TEE, ≥30-d rhythm monitoring, hypercoagulable/VTE testing as indicated).

- Greatest benefit with **atrial septal aneurysm or large shunt **, high RoPE (≥7), and PASCAL “probable/highly likely.”

- SCAI 2022 guideline: case-by-case above 60.

- Device-related AF is mostly early and transient. Poor Valsalva under sedation can miss a shunt on TEE. **Non-stroke indications: platypnea-orthodeoxia** (dyspnea/desaturation **upright **, relieved supine); decompression illness in divers; paradoxical systemic embolism. Migraine is not an indication (PREMIUM, PRIMA).

### Ventricular septal defect

- **Closure:** Qp:Qs ≥1.5 with LV volume overload and PASP <50% systemic / PVR <⅓ systemic; prior endocarditis; residual post-surgical defect.

- Perimembranous device closure risks **CHB **. Muscular defects are device-friendly.

- Post-MI VSR → surgery (device for residual or bridging). Long-standing VSD → RV overload/PH; step-up at the RV level.

### Pulmonary stenosis and RVOT

- **Valvular PS** is the most common isolated obstructive CHD (~7–10%); post-stenotic PA dilation.

- **Severity:** moderate peak gradient 36–64 mmHg; severe >64 mmHg (mean >35).

**Balloon valvuloplasty** for symptomatic moderate–severe or asymptomatic severe **domed** PS. Dysplastic valve (Noonan), sub/supravalvular stenosis, hypoplastic annulus or severe PR → surgery. **Complications:**

   - PR

   - Ectopy, RBBB/AVB

   - Perforation

   - **“Suicide RV”** (dynamic infundibular obstruction after relief): IV fluids + BB; avoid inotropes

- **Repaired ToF** (PR → RV dilation → dysfunction/VT): **PVR (surgical or transcatheter)** for symptomatic ≥moderate PR.

   - Asymptomatic: RV dilation ( **RVESVi >80 mL/m²** emphasized in 2025 ACHD; RVEDVi ≥160, RV:LV EDV ≥2), RV/LV dysfunction, RVOT obstruction (RVSP ≥⅔ systemic), progressive exercise intolerance, sustained arrhythmia.

- **TPVR:** Melody and SAPIEN (conduits/bioprostheses); Harmony and Alterra (native/patched RVOT). **Before TPVR: coronary compression testing** (balloon in RVOT with simultaneous coronary angiography; anomalous coronaries are common in ToF). Stent the conduit first to prevent fracture. Watch for aortic root compression.

### Coarctation of the aorta

- **Associations: 50–75% of CoA patients have BAV **; Turner syndrome (~12–15%); Shone complex (supramitral ring, parachute MV, subaortic stenosis, CoA); intracranial aneurysm (~10%, screen).

- **Findings:** upper-extremity HTN; arm–leg BP gradient; radio-femoral delay; interscapular murmur; rib notching and “3 sign”; diastolic runoff (continuous forward flow) in the descending/abdominal aorta.

- **Imaging:** CMR or CTA for all repaired and unrepaired patients (Class 1).

- **Intervention** (with HTN): peak-to-peak gradient ≥20 mmHg, or <20 mmHg with significant anatomic narrowing plus collaterals, LV dysfunction or AR. **Stenting (covered stent preferred in adults:** COAST/COAST II) beats balloon alone (aneurysm, restenosis).

   - Balloon should not exceed the diameter of the adjacent normal aorta (at the diaphragm). Stent should be expandable to adult size (≥20 mm). Staged re-dilation for severe lesions.

- **Complications:** dissection/rupture (have covered stents ready), aneurysm, stent migration, femoral access injury.

## 13. Hemodynamics and Cath-Lab Pearls

### Calculations

**Mixed venous O₂ (Flamm)** = (3 × SVC + IVC) / 4.

- **Qp:Qs** = (SaO₂ − MvO₂) / (PvO₂ − PaO₂). Use SaO₂ for PvO₂ if not sampled (unless there is a right-to-left shunt). **Significant step-up:** ≥7% at atrial level, ≥5% at ventricle or PA.

- **SVR** = 80 × (MAP − RAP) / CO. **PVR (WU)** = (mPAP − PCWP) / CO.

- **CPO** = MAP × CO / 451. **PAPi** = (PASP − PADP) / RAP. **Before closing a left-to-right shunt:** Qp:Qs ≥1.5, PASP <50% systemic, PVR <⅓ SVR (or low WU per lesion). **Gorlin:** AVA = CO/(HR × SEP) / (44.3 × √MG). **Hakki:** AVA ≈ CO / √(peak-to-peak gradient).

### Pressure-waveform diagnoses

- **Tamponade:** equalized diastolic pressures (RA ≈ RVEDP ≈ PCWP), pulsus paradoxus >10 mmHg, blunted y descent. **Constriction vs restriction**

   - Both: dip-and-plateau, equalization within 5 mmHg, Kussmaul sign.

   - **Constriction-specific: ventricular interdependence **, meaning discordant RV/LV systolic pressures with respiration (inspiration: RV ↑, LV ↓); **systolic area index >1.1 **; RVEDP/RVSP >⅓. **Restriction:** concordant pressures; PASP >55–60; LVEDP − RVEDP >5.

- **Severe MR:** giant v wave on PCWP/LA. After PMBC, a new tall v wave = iatrogenic MR.

- **HCM:** Brockenbrough sign. **AS:** post-PVC pulse pressure ↑; Carabello sign (↑ arterial pressure on crossing the valve).

- **Coronary damping or ventricularization:** ostial lesion, spasm, non-coaxial or deep catheter. Don’t inject; pull back.

### Ventriculography and injections

- **RAO 30°:** anterior, apical, inferior walls and MR. **LAO 45– 60° ± cranial:** septum, lateral wall, VSD.

- **Rate:** ~30–45 mL at 10–15 mL/s via pigtail. Use <10 mL/s via end-hole catheters.

   - **Avoid or reduce with:** LVEDP/PCWP ≥25 (pulmonary edema; pretreat NTG/nitroprusside), critical LM, severe AS, LV thrombus.

   - **Pigtail position:** avoid entrapment in trabeculae (staining, VT). Warning signs: close to inferior wall, loop distorts in systole, pigtail unfurls on withdrawal. RV angiography always causes ectopy.

- **Catheter selection**

   - **Left-sided SVGs:** JR4, LCB, AL1 (upward take-off: LCB, hockey stick).

   - **Right-sided SVGs** (downward): MP, JR4, RCB.

   - **LIMA:** IMA or JR4 from the vertical subclavian (Bernstein/vertebral to enter the subclavian).

   - **RCA:** anterior or high take-off → AR, hockey stick, AL. Inferior/vertical → MP. Superior course → IM, hockey stick.

   - **LCA:** dilated root → JL5/JL6; small root → JL3–3.5.

   - **Post-TAVR:** Evolut → JL3.5 / JR4 through frame cells ± guide extension. SAPIEN → JL4 or EBU 3.5–3.75, JR4. **Guide-extension or deep-engagement dissection:** Amplatz + RCA is the classic culprit.

- **Anatomy and venous pearls**

   - **CFA bifurcation** is below the femoral head in ~65% and below its center in ~95%.

   - **Persistent left SVC** drains to a dilated coronary sinus behind the LA. Coronary sinus diverticula are linked to accessory pathways and SCD.

- **Azygos** runs posteriorly along the spine. **Scimitar:** anomalous right PV to IVC. A Swan catheter outside the cardiac border may be in an anomalous vein.

- **After transseptal puncture,** park the wire in the left upper PV. Sudden collapse after PFO/ASD/LAAO → suspect LAA/LA perforation.

- **PV stenosis after ablation** (<1%): dyspnea, hemoptysis; balloon ± stent.

- **SVC syndrome** (malignant): stent, with radiation as indicated.

- **Periprocedural:**

   - NPO ~2 h clears / 6 h solids for sedation (not in emergencies).

   - Pregnancy test in women of child-bearing potential. Half-dose basal insulin the night before; hold oral agents the morning of; early case slot.

- **Repaired CHD** (root or coronary manipulation, e.g., arterial switch) with angina → coronary evaluation (CCTA or angiography).

- **Repaired CHD with QRS widening or syncope** → EP referral.

- Endomyocardial biopsy tamponade ~0.5–1%.

- **Acute aortic dissection:** SBP <120 mmHg and HR <60 (IV BB first, then vasodilator). Acute mesenteric ischemia: pain out of proportion to exam.

## 14. Statistics

**Test performance**

|**Term**|**Formula**|
|---|---|
|Sensitivity|TP / (TP + FN), among diseased|
|Specificity|TN / (TN + FP), among non-diseased|
|PPV|TP / (TP + FP)|
|NPV|TN / (TN + FN)|
|LR+ / LR−|Sens/(1−Spec); (1−Sens)/Spec|

- **Sensitivity and specificity are fixed properties of the test. PPV and NPV depend on prevalence (Bayes).**

- **Example** (sensitivity 90%, specificity 90%, n = 1000):

   - Prevalence 10%: PPV 50%, NPV 99% (a positive test is unhelpful; a negative rules out). Prevalence 90%: PPV 99%, NPV 50%.

   - Prevalence 50%: PPV = NPV = 90%.

- Testing is most useful at **intermediate pretest probability **. Pretest probability (history, exam, ECG) is the most important input.

- **Verification bias:** short follow-up misclassifies false negatives as true negatives and inflates apparent NPV.

### Effect measures

   - **RR** = risk in treated / risk in control. **RRR** = 1 − RR. **ARR** = risk(control) − risk(treated). **NNT** = 1/ARR. **Lytics example:** mortality 11.6% → 8.7%. ARR 2.9%, RRR 25%, NNT ≈ 34 (29 lives per 1000).

   - **GLOBAL LEADERS example:** 3.8% vs 4.4%. RR 0.87, RRR 13%, ARR 0.6%, NNT ≈ 167 (not significant).

   - **Odds ratio** = odds(treated) / odds(control). Approximates RR only when events are rare. Used in case-control designs.

   - **Hazard ratio:** time-to-event (Cox). **Win ratio:** hierarchical composite ranking of pairs, prioritizing death (e.g., CHIPBCIS3).

### Inference and design

- **p-value:** probability of data this extreme if the null is true. **95% CI:** precision; excluding 1.0 (ratio) or 0 (difference) = significant. More events give narrower CIs. **Type I error (α)** = false positive. **Type II (β)** = false negative. **Power** = 1 − β (↑ with sample size, event rate, effect size).

- **Superiority vs noninferiority:** NI tests whether the upper CI bound stays below a prespecified margin. A trial can show NI and then superiority if the CI lies entirely on the benefit side.

- **Crossover, non-adherence and low event rates bias toward the null.** In superiority trials that is conservative; in NI trials it is anti-conservative, so review per-protocol analyses too.

- **ITT** analyzes patients as randomized (preserves randomization). **Per-protocol/as-treated** analyzes what patients received (biased). Example: CABANA ITT neutral, as-treated favored ablation.

- **Designs:** RCT (investigator assigns; randomization removes selection bias). Cohort (defined by exposure; prospective/retrospective). Case-control (defined by outcome).

- **Propensity scores** balance measured, not unmeasured, confounders.

- **Surrogate endpoint:** biomarker substituting for a clinical outcome (smaller trials, possibly misleading). **Composite endpoint:** ↑ power, but components may differ in importance and direction.

- **Equipoise:** genuine uncertainty that justifies randomization.

- **Epidemiology:** incidence = new cases over time; prevalence = existing cases at a point in time; case-fatality = deaths among those with the disease.
